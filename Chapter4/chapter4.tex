In this chapter, we present a unified variational framework for sampling and generative modelling centred around divergences on path measures. In the first section, we present a novel theoretical result in \Cref{prop:fbrnd} that allows us to define the path density ratio between forward and backward path measures. With this novel density ratio, we are then able to define divergences between arbitrary forward and backward path measures. We then show how this framework can be used to unify existing sampling and generative modelling methods in \Cref{sec:connections_to_existing_methodologies}.

In \Cref{sec:controlled_monte_carlo_diffusions}, we will revisit the Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) algorithm under our framework, and show that it has some conceptual issues. We then propose a control-based approach using our novel density ratio, which we term \textit{Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusions} (CMCD). The CMCD algorithm is a score-based technique that adapts both the forward and backward dynamics of a diffusion to match the target distribution, as well as intermediate annealed distributions. This allows us to learn a more accurate proposal distribution which has lowered variance in the importance sampling estimate, compared to AIS (and other extensions). We will then propose an efficient machine learning algorithm in \Cref{alg:kl_cmcd} for learning the importance sampling weights of CMCD in an end-to-end manner, and demonstrate the effectiveness of this algorithm on unnormalised density estimation tasks across a range of synthetic and real-world datasets.

\section{A Path-Based Unifying Variational Framework}
\label{sec:path_unifying_framework}
Let us revisit the framework for unnormalised density estimation and generative modelling that we introduced in \Cref{sec:unifying_framework_discrete}. 

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Choosing an Agnostic Notation}
    Unlike in \Cref{sec:unifying_framework_discrete}, we don't immediately make a distinction between a ``simple'' distribution $\mu$ and a distribution of interest $\pi$. Instead, we consider a more general notation where we consider two arbitrary probability distributions $\mu$ and $\nu$, and redefine the framework to consider the task of transporting $\mu$ to $\nu$ and vice-versa. We choose to use an agnostic notation where we don't make a distinction between the forward and backward processes, and show in due time that by making specific choices for what $\mu$ and $\nu$ are defined to be, we can recover both the generative modelling and sampling frameworks.
\end{lightgraybox}

We consider methodologies which can be implemented by the following generative process\label{ntn:base_distbn_cmcd}
\[
    z \sim \nu(z), \quad x \mid z \sim p_{\theta}(x \mid z). \label{eq:unifying_framework_discrete}
\]
which transforms a sample $z$ from a distribution $\nu$ to a sample $x \sim \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z)  \dd \mu(z)$. If we require that the marginal at $x$ matches the distribution $\mu$, we can see that $\mu(x) = \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \dd \nu(z)$.\label{ntn:mappings}
% The tasks of sampling and generative modelling can then be framed as learning the transition density $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ that maps samples from the base distribution $\mu$ to the distribution of interest $\pi$. Traditionally, $\mu(z)$ is a simple auxiliary distribution, and the family of transitions $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ is parameterised flexibly and in such a way that sampling according to \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_discrete} is tractable. Then we can frame the tasks of generative modelling and sampling as finding transition densities such that the marginal at $x$ matches the target distribution,
Equivalently, and without loss of generality, we can define the reverse generative process as well, which takes a sample $x$ from the distribution $\mu$ and maps it to a sample $z \sim q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ from $\nu$. 
% For example, it is often easier to define a way to iteratively add noise to $x$ to get a sample from $\mu$, than it is to directly define the transition density $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$. 
We introduce the reverse process as
\[
x \sim \mu(x), \quad z \mid x \sim q_{\phi}(z \mid x). \label{eq:unifying_framework_reverse}
\]
relying on an appropriately parameterised backward transition $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$. We will say that \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_discrete} and \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_reverse} are reversals of each other in the case when their joint distributions coincide, that is, when
\[
    q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mu(x) = p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \nu(z).
\]
Building on this observation, it is natural to define the loss function
\[
    \mathcal{L}_D(\phi, \theta) := D\left(q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mu(x) \| p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \nu(z)\right), \label{eq:unifying_framework_loss}
\]
where $D$ is a divergence between the forward and backward path measures. This leads us to the following framework, which we described briefly in \Cref{sec:unifying_framework_discrete},

\begin{selfframework}{}
    \label{framework:unifying_framework_discrete}
    Let $D$ be an arbitrary divergence, and assume that $\mathcal{L}_D(\phi, \theta)=0$ in \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_loss}. Then we have  
    \[
        \mu(x) = \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \dd \nu(z), \quad \nu(z) = \int q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \dd \mu(x), \label{eq:unifying_framework_discrete_result}
    \]
    that is, $\nu(z)$ is transformed into $\mu(x)$ by $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$, and $\mu(x)$ is transformed into $\nu(z)$ by $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$.
\end{selfframework}

\subsection{A Choice of Parameterisation: Hierarchical VAEs}

As mentioned in \Cref{sec:unifying_framework_discrete}, a particularly flexible choice of parametrisation is to implicitly parameterise $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ and $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ via a hierarchical model with intermediate latents\footnote{We not that this is not a loss of generality, as we can always swap our notation for $\mu$ and $\nu$, as well as $x$ and $z$, as there are no additional assumptions about these distributions.}: We identify $x=: x_0$ and $z=: x_T$ as ``endpoints'' of the layered augmentation $\left\{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}, x_T\right\}=: x_{0: T}$. The hierarchical latent variable model is then defined as
\[
    q_{\phi}\left(x_T, \ldots, x_1 | x_0\right) := \prod_{t=1}^T q_{\phi_{t-1}}\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right), \quad p_\theta\left(x_0, \ldots, x_{T-1} | x_T\right):=\prod_{t=1}^T p_{\theta_{t-1}}\left(x_{t-1} | x_t\right). \label{eq:hierarchical_latent}
\]
We can therefore recover $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ and $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ by marginalising out the intermediate latents $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}\}$ in \Cref{eq:hierarchical_latent}. In the regime when $T$ is large, the models in \Cref{eq:hierarchical_latent} are very expressive, even if the intermediate transition kernels are parameterised in a simple manner. We hence proceed by assuming Gaussian distributions,\label{ntn:gaussian_latents}
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
q_{\phi_{t-1}}\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right) &= \mathcal{N}\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}+\delta a_{\phi_{t-1}}\left(x_{t-1}\right), \delta \sigma^2 I\right), \label{eq:gaussian_fwd}\\
p_{\theta_t}\left(x_{t-1} \mid x_t\right) &= \mathcal{N}\left(x_{t-1} \mid x_t+\delta b_{\theta_t}\left(x_t\right), \delta \sigma^2 I\right), \label{eq:gaussian_rev}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\sigma>0$ controls the standard deviation, and $\delta>0$ is small\label{ntn:std_dev_gaussian_latent}\label{ntn:small_delta}. The vector fields $a_{\phi_t}\left(x_t\right)$ and $b_{\theta_t}\left(x_t\right)$ introduced in \Cref{eq:gaussian_fwd,eq:gaussian_rev} are parameterised by $\phi$ and $\theta$, but we will suppress this explicit dependence if it is clear from the context\label{ntn:vector_fields}. We further define notation for the joint forward and backward probability distributions,
\[
q_{\mu, \phi}\left(x_{0:T}\right):=q_{\phi}\left(x_{1:T} \mid x_0\right) \mu\left(x_0\right) \quad \text{and} \quad p_{\nu, \theta}\left(x_{0:T}\right):=p_{\theta}\left(x_{0:T-1} \mid x_T\right) \nu\left(x_T\right),
\]
and think of those implied joint distributions as emanating from $\mu(x)=\mu\left(x_0\right)$ and $\nu(z)=\nu\left(x_T\right)$, respectively, moving \textit{forwards} or \textit{backwards} according to the specific choices for $\phi$ and $\theta$. The model in \Cref{eq:hierarchical_latent} is equivalently defined via the Markov chains
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
x_{t+1} &\leftarrow x_t+\delta a_{\phi_t}\left(x_t\right)+ \sqrt{\delta}\sigma_t \varepsilon_{t+1}, & x_0 \sim \mu \Longrightarrow x_{0: T} \sim q_{\mu, \phi}\left(x_{0: T}\right), \label{eq:mc_framework_fwd_ch4} \\
x_{t-1} &\leftarrow x_t+ \delta b_{\theta_t}\left(x_t\right)+ \sqrt{\delta}\sigma_t \varepsilon_t, & x_T \sim \nu \Longrightarrow x_{0: T} \sim p_{\nu, \theta}\left(x_{0: T}\right), \label{eq:mc_framework_rev_ch4}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\left\{\varepsilon_t\right\}_{t=1}^T$ are i.i.d. standard normal random variables. We note that in this hierarchical setup, the transition densities of interest, i.e. $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ and $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$, are not available in closed form. However, generalising slightly from \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_discrete}, we could instead minimise the extended loss
\[
\mathcal{L}_D^{\operatorname{ext}}(\phi, \theta)=D\left(q_{\mu, \phi}\left(x_{0:T}\right) \| p_{\nu, \theta}\left(x_{0: T}\right)\right),
\]
where here, $D$ is a divergence on the \textit{discrete path space} $\left\{x_{0:T}\right\}$. Clearly, $\mathcal{L}_D^{\text {ext }}(\phi, \theta)=0$ still implies \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_discrete_result}, but is no longer equivalent. More specifically, in the case when $D=D_{\mathrm{KL}}$, the data processing inequality \parencite{cover1999elements} yields
\[
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\mu, \phi}\left(x_{0:T}\right) \| p_{\nu, \theta}\left(x_{0: T}\right)\right) \geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mu(x) \| p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z)\right),
\]
so that $\mathcal{L}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}^{\text {ext}}(\phi, \theta)$ provides an upper bound for $\mathcal{L}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}(\phi, \theta)$ as defined in \Cref{eq:unifying_framework_loss}.

\subsection{Diffusions: Hierarchical VAEs in the Infinite-Depth Limit}

Taking inspiration from \textcite{tzen2019neural, li2020scalable, huang2021variational}, we consider the infinite-depth limit of the hierarchical VAEs, where the number of layers $T$ goes to infinity. The discrete paths $\{x_{0:T}\}$ give rise to continuous paths $\left(X_t\right)_{0 \leq t \leq T} \in$ $C\left([0, T] ; \mathbb{R}^d\right)$\footnote{Here, $C\left([0, T] ; \mathbb{R}^d\right)$ denotes the space of continuous functions from the interval $[0, T]$ to $\mathbb{R}^d$.}. To complete the set-up, we think of $a_{\phi} = (a_{\phi_0}, \ldots, a_{\phi_{T-1}})$ and $b_{\theta} = (b_{\theta_1}, \ldots, b_{\theta_T})$ in \Cref{eq:mc_framework_fwd_ch4,eq:mc_framework_rev_ch4} as arising from time-dependent vector fields $a, b \in C^{\infty}\left([0, T] \times \mathbb{R}^d ; \mathbb{R}^d\right)$. Taking the limit $\delta \rightarrow 0$, while keeping $T>0$ fixed, transforms the Markov chains in \Cref{eq:mc_framework_fwd_ch4,eq:mc_framework_rev_ch4} into continuous-time dynamics described by the SDEs \parencite{tzen2019neural}:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
 \dd X_t = a_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \dd W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \mu \Longrightarrow (X_t)_{0 \leq t \leq T} \sim \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}, \label{eq:forward_diffusion_process} \\
 \dd X_t = b_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \dd \overleftarrow{W}_t, \quad X_T \sim \nu \Longrightarrow (X_t)_{0 \leq t \leq T} \sim \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}, \label{eq:backward_diffusion_process}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $W_t$ and $\overleftarrow{W}_t$ are standard Brownian motions in the forward and backward directions, respectively (see \Cref{def:nelson-reversal} for notation and definitions). Therefore, from \Cref{def:path_measure_of_ito_process}, we see that \Cref{eq:forward_diffusion_process} and \Cref{eq:backward_diffusion_process} induce the path measures $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}$ and $\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}$ on the path space $C\left([0, T] ; \mathbb{R}^d\right)$. 
\begin{lightgraybox}{Notation for Path Measures}
    We will be using the notation for path measures introduced in \Cref{eq:forward_diffusion_process} and \Cref{eq:backward_diffusion_process} continuously going forward, and it is important to make clear the notation being used.
    \[
    \nonumber
    \overset{\badge{yellow!30}{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{P}}
        ^{\badge{blue!20}{\mu},\,\badge{red!20}{a}}
        _{\badge{green!20}{t}} \label{eq:notation_for_path_measures}
    \]

    \begin{enumerate}
    \item \(\badge{yellow!30}{\longrightarrow}\)\,:  \textbf{orientation arrow} – indicates the time direction of the path measure, whether it is forward or backward.
    \item \(\badge{blue!20}{\mu}\)\,:  \textbf{initial law} – the probability distribution of the starting point \(X_0\) (for a path measure going forward) or of \(X_T\) (for a path measure going backward).
    \item \(\badge{red!20}{a}\)\,:   \textbf{vector field/drift} – the vector field or drift term that drives the SDE corresponding to the path measure. Occasionally, when it is not clear from the context, we will also include the parameterisation of the vector field, i.e. $a_{\phi}$.
    \item \(\badge{green!20}{t}\)\,: \textbf{time index (optional)}  – used when referring to the marginal of the path measure at a fixed time \(t\). Without a subscript, it is assumed that we are referring to the joint path measure over the entire time interval.
    \end{enumerate}
    Further, we will refer to the time-marginal and conditional distributions of the path measure as \label{ntn:path_measure_time_marginal}\label{ntn:path_measure_conditional_distribution}
    \[
        \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_t^{\mu, f} =: \rho_t^{\mu, f}, \\
        \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}(X_t \mid X_s = x) =: \rho_{t \mid s}^{\mu, f}(\cdot \mid x).
    \]
\end{lightgraybox}
We maintain the relations $X_0=x$ and $X_T=z$, and the transitions are parameterised by the vector fields $a_{\phi}, b_{\theta}$, in the following sense: 
\[
    p_{\theta}(x \mid z) &= \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}_0^{\nu, b_{\theta}}\left(x \mid X_T=z\right) := \rho_{0|T}^{\nu, b_{\theta}} (x \mid z), \\
    q_{\phi}(z \mid x) &= \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_T^{\mu, a_{\phi}}\left(z \mid X_0=x\right) := \rho_{T|0}^{\mu, a_{\phi}}(z \mid x).
\]
We rewrite \Cref{def:nelson-reversal} \parencite{anderson1982reverse,nelson2020dynamical} in terms of path measures that is more convenient for our purposes:
\begin{selfproposition}[Time-reversal of SDEs with reverse time]
    \label{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes}
    For $\mu$ and $a$ of sufficient regularity, denote the time-marginals of the corresponding path measure by $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_t^{\mu, a} =: \rho_t^{\mu, a}$. Then $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a} = \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}$ if and only if
    \[
        \nu = \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_T^{\mu, a} \quad \text { and } \quad b_t=a_t-\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \rho_t^{\mu, a}, \quad \text { for all } t \in(0, T].
    \]
\end{selfproposition}
% NOTE: Maybe add this back in Chapter 3 when defining time reversal.

We can then write our framework in terms of the infinite-depth, SDE-based parametrisation as
\begin{selfframework}{}
    \label{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}
    Let $D$ be a divergence on path spaces, and define the loss function
    \[
        \mathcal{L}_D(\phi, \theta)=D\left( \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a_{\phi}} \| \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b_{\theta}} \right) \label{eq:loss_cont_kl}.
    \]
    If $\mathcal{L}_D(\phi, \theta)=0$, then we have that \Cref{eq:forward_diffusion_process} transports $\mu$ to $\nu$ under forward dynamics, and \Cref{eq:backward_diffusion_process} transports $\nu$ to $\mu$ under backward dynamics.
\end{selfframework}

However, as we discussed in \Cref{sec:time_reversal_of_sdes}, the current stochastic calculus framework under It{\^o}'s formula does not allow us to easily write down the path density ratio between forward and backward path measures. For this reason, we rely on the mathematical framework of F{\"o}llmer's Itô calculus \parencite{eberle2011introduction, follmer2006calcul} from \Cref{sec:folmer_ito_calculus} to define a novel Radon-Nikodym derivative between the forward and backward path measures:

\begin{selfproposition}[Forward-backward Radon-Nikodym derivative]
    \label{prop:fbrnd}
    Let $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\Gamma_0, \gamma^{+}} = \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\Gamma_T, \gamma^{-}}$ be a reference path measure (that is, $\Gamma_0, \Gamma_T$ and $\gamma^{ \pm}$ define diffusions as in \Cref{eq:forward_diffusion_process} and \Cref{eq:backward_diffusion_process} and are related as in \Cref{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes}), which is absolutely continuous with respect to both $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, f}$ and $\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}$. Then, $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}$-almost surely, the corresponding Radon-Nikodym derivative is given by\label{ntn:fbrnd}\label{ntn:fwd_reference}\label{ntn:bwd_reference}
    \begin{subequations}
        \label{eq:fbrnd}
    \begin{align}
        \ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}}{\mathrm{~d} \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}}\right)(X) & =\ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \mu}{\mathrm{~d} \Gamma_0}\right)\left(X_0\right)-\ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \nu}{\mathrm{~d} \Gamma_T}\right)\left(X_T\right) \label{eq:fbrnd_boundary} \\
        & +\int_0^T \frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\left(a_t-\gamma_t^{+}\right)\left(X_t\right) \cdot\left(\mathrm{d} X_t-\frac{1}{2}\left(a_t+\gamma_t^{+}\right)\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t\right) \label{eq:fbrnd_fwd} \\
        & -\int_0^T \frac{1}{\sigma_t^2}\left(b_t-\gamma_t^{-}\right)\left(X_t\right) \cdot\left(\mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{X}_t-\frac{1}{2}\left(b_t+\gamma_t^{-}\right)\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t\right) \label{eq:fbrnd_bwd}.
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
\end{selfproposition}
\begin{proofsketch}
    In order to define an RND between a forward and a reverse path measure, we take the following steps:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Introduce a simple, reversible, ``reference'' path measure $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\Gamma_0, \gamma^{+}} = \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\Gamma_T, \gamma^{-}}$. For example, if $\Gamma_0$ and $\Gamma_T$ are both Gaussian and $\gamma^{+}$ and $\gamma^{-}$ are linear drift terms, then this reference is easily reversible. 
        \item Define a reversal operator $\mathcal{R}$ that acts on individual sample paths in a F{\"o}llmer-Itô sense (see \Cref{sec:folmer_ito_calculus}). This allows us to be able to transform the RND between two forward path measures into an RND between two reverse path measures. 
        \item Use the multiplicative decomposition of an RND to write down the RND between a forward and reverse path measure as the product of the RND between the forward path measure and the forward reference process, with the RND of the backward path measure and the backward reference process.
    \end{itemize}
\end{proofsketch}

See Appendix \ref{sec:proof-fbrnd-app}.
\end{proof}

\begin{selfremark}[Role of the Reference Process]
    According to \Cref{prop:fbrnd}, the Radon-Nikodym derivative between $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}$ and $\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}$ can be decomposed into boundary terms (\Cref{eq:fbrnd_boundary}), as well as forward and backward path integrals (\Cref{eq:fbrnd_fwd}, \Cref{eq:fbrnd_bwd}). Since the left-hand side of \Cref{eq:fbrnd} does not depend on the reference $\Gamma_{0, T}, \gamma^{ \pm}$, the expressions in \Cref{eq:fbrnd} are in principle equivalent for all choices of reference. The freedom in $\Gamma_{0, T}$ and $\gamma^{ \pm}$ allows us to 'reweight' between \Cref{eq:fbrnd_boundary}, \Cref{eq:fbrnd_fwd} and \Cref{eq:fbrnd_bwd}, or even cancel terms. A canonical choice is the Lebesgue measure for $\Gamma_0$ and $\Gamma_T$, and $\gamma^{ \pm}=0$.

\end{selfremark}

\begin{selfremark}[Forward and Backward Integrals, and their Conversion]
    \label{rem:conversion}
    The distinction between forward and backward integration in \Cref{eq:fbrnd} is related to the time points at which the integrands $\left(a_t-\gamma_t^{+}\right)\left(X_t\right)$ and $\left(b_t-\gamma_t^{-}\right)\left(X_t\right)$ would be evaluated in discrete-time approximations. We discuss this thoroughly in \Cref{prop:solutions-for-forward-and-reverse-processes}, especially in \Cref{eq:forward-ito-integral-limit} and \Cref{eq:reverse-ito-integral-limit}. Using those equations gives us
    \[
    &\int_0^T a_t\left(X_t\right) \cdot \mathrm{d} X_t \approx \sum_i a_{t_i}\left(X_{t_i}\right) \cdot\left(X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_i}\right), \\
    &\int_0^T a_t\left(X_t\right) \cdot \mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{X}_t \approx \sum_i a_{t_{i+1}}\left(X_{t_{i+1}}\right) \cdot\left(X_{t_{i+1}}-X_{t_i}\right).
    \]
    Similarly, forward and backward integrals can be transformed into each other using the conversion formulas in \Cref{eq:forward-reverse-ito-conversion} and \Cref{eq:reverse-forward-ito-conversion}.
\end{selfremark}

\section{Connections to Existing Methodologies}
\label{sec:connections_to_existing_methodologies}

Armed with the forward-backward RND from \Cref{prop:fbrnd}, we can now show how the proposed framework unifies a large family of existing methods, across generative modelling and sampling-based inference, simply through specific choices of the divergence $D$, the two distributions $\mu$ and $\nu$, and the reference process $\Gamma_0, \Gamma_T, \gamma^{ \pm}$.

\subsection{Generative Modelling}

Considering the generative modelling setting discussed in \Cref{sec:gen_modelling}, we are interested in a situation where we have access to a training dataset $\Dtrain = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}$ of samples from the unknown density $\pi$, and we wish to be able to obtain new samples from $\pi$ through a parametrised mapping to a latent space $z$. Specifically, in this section, we consider the score-based generative modelling approaches introduced in \Cref{sec:sm} and \Cref{sec:ddpm}. 

\textbf{Implicit Score Matching.} Under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we make the choice of $\mu = \pi$ and $\nu = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, and fix the forward drift $a_t$ to refer to a simple ``noising'' diffusion (such as in \Cref{eq:sm_sde_fwd} or \Cref{eq:ddpm_sde_fwd}) that takes a sample $x \sim \mu$ and noises it until it approaches Gaussian noise (i.e. $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$). By making this choice of $a_t$, we circumvent the non-uniqueness of \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}. We further decide to parametrise the backwards drift $b_{\theta, t} = a_t - s_{\theta, t}$ through some parametrised function $s_{\theta}$ that we wish to learn using our framework\label{ntn:score_net}. We make some ergodicity assumptions here about the forward drift $a_t$, asusming that when $T > 0$, the forward SDE converges to an invariant distribution that is close to $\nu = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. Under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, when the divergence measure is minimised, we have that $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a} = \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b_{\theta}}$, and therefore from \Cref{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes} we see that the minimisation of the divergence measure results in $s_{\theta, t} = \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \rho_t^{\mu, a}$.

\begin{selfproposition}[Implicit Score Matching from \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}]
    \label{prop:score_matching_from_framework}
    The implicit score matching loss from \textcite[Theorem 1, Equation (4)]{hyvarinen2005estimation} is equivalent to the KL divergence between a forward noising diffusion path measure and its time reversal under \Cref{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes}:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ISM}}(s) &:= D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}| | \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, a - s_{\theta}}\right), \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}}\left[\frac{1}{2} \int_0^T s_{\theta, t}^2\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t+\int_0^T\left(\nabla \cdot s_{\theta, t}\right)\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t\right]+\text { const.} \label{eq:ism_loss_equivalence}
\]
\end{selfproposition}
\begin{proof}
    See Appendix \ref{sec:proof-ism-from-framework}.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Denoising Score Matching.} By using integration by parts, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{ISM}}$ is equivalent to Denoising Score Matching \parencite{song2019generative, song2020score}:
\[
 D_{\text{KL}}(\fwdP{\mu}{a} \| \bwdP{\nu}{a-s}) = \E_{X \sim \fwdP{\mu}{a}} \left[ \frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \int_0^T \|  s_{\theta, t}(X_t) - \nabla \rho_{t \mid 0}^{\mu, a} (X_t \mid X_0)\|^2 \mathrm{d} t \right] + \text{const.}
\]
We can even accommodate modifications of \Cref{eq:ism_loss_equivalence}; in particular the divergence term in \Cref{eq:ism_loss_equivalence} can be replaced by a backward integral, using the conversion formula from \Cref{cor:relating-forward-and-reverse-ito-integrals-through-divergence}. We also note that the settings discussed in this section are similar to the formulations in \textcite{kingma2021variational, huang2021variational}.
Finally, we highlight that this framework is not limited to ergodic models and can in fact accommodate finite time models\footnote{Models where the forward drift is guaranteed to hit $\nu$ at a finite time $T$ and not merely in an ergodic sense with large $T \to \infty$.} by using a Doob's $h$-transform based SDE for $\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a}$ as opposed to the classical VP-SDE (see \textcite[Example 2.4, Pg. 3]{ye2022first} and \textcite{rogers2000diffusions}).

\subsection{Unnormalised Density Sampling}
\label{sec:unnormalised_density_sampling_cmcd}
Considering the unnormalised density sampling setting discussed in \Cref{sec:unnormalised_density_sampling}, we now have a siutation where we can evaluate the unknown density $\pi$ upto a normalising constant (i.e. $\pi = \hpi / Z$), and we wish to learn a parametrised mapping that allows us to draw samples from $\hpi$, in order to estimate expectations and the normalising constant $Z$. Specifically, in this section, we consider score-based sampling approaches, as well as AIS-based approaches. Under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we make the choice of $\nu = \hpi$, and $\mu$ a tractable distribution of our choice (e.g. $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$). We further fix the backward drift $b_t$ to a standard SDE such as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, in order to circumvent the non-uniqueness of \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}. The forward drift is parametrised as $a_{\theta, t}$, and we learn $\theta$ by minimising $D(\fwdP{\mu}{a_{\theta}}\|\bwdP{\nu}{b})$.

\textbf{Ergodic diffusions}. Algorithms such as Denoising Diffusion Sampler (DDS, \textcite{vargas2023denoising}), Diffusion Sampler (DIS, \textcite{berner2022optimal}) fix a backward drift $b_t$ that induces an ergodic backward diffusion\footnote{An ergodic SDE that converges to an invariant distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ at $T \to \infty$, but is close enough in finite time.}, so that for large $T$, the marginal at initial time $\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{0}^{\nu, b}$ is close to the corresponding invariant distribution, and in particular (almost) independent of $\nu$. Defining $\mu := \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{0}^{\nu, b}$, and parametrising the forward drift $a_t := b_t + \sigma^2 s_{\theta, t}$, \textcite{vargas2023denoising, berner2022optimal} set out to minimise the denoising diffusion sampler loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS}}$. 
\begin{selfproposition}[DDS and DIS from \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}]
The loss function $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS}}$ from \textcite[Equation (10)]{vargas2023denoising} is equivalent to the KL divergence between the path measure of an SDE parametrised by a forward drift $a_t := b_t + \sigma^2 s_{\theta, t}$ and a reverse path measure parametrised by drift $b_t$:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDS}}(s) &:=D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, b + \sigma^2 s_{\theta}} \| \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}\right), \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{X \sim \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, b+\sigma^2 s_{\theta}}}\left[\sigma^2 \int_0^T s_{\theta, t}^2\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t+\ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \Gamma_T}{\mathrm{~d} \nu}\right)\left(X_T\right)\right]. \label{eq:kl_sampling}
\]
\end{selfproposition}
\begin{proof}
    We choose the reference process to be $\Gamma_{0, T}=\mu$, $\gamma^{ \pm}=b$ (that is, the reference process is at stationarity, with invariant measure $\mu$), and recover the above from direct calculation based on \Cref{eq:fbrnd}.
\end{proof}
Finally we note that whilst the work in \textcite{berner2022optimal} focuses on exploring a VP-SDE-based approach which is ergodic, their overarching framework generalises beyond ergodic settings. This objective is the same as the KL expressions in \textcite[Proposition 1]{vargas2021machine} and \textcite[Proposition 9]{liu2022deep}.

\textbf{F{\"o}llmer drift.} Instead of considering an ergodic backwards diffusion which only has guarantees of converging to an invariant distribution $\mu$ at large $T$ and with sufficiently large $b_t$, one can also consider a finite-time hitting algorithm. Choosing $b_t(x) = x / t$, one can show using Doob's $h$-transform \parencite[Theorem 40.3(iii)]{rogers2000diffusions} that $\overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}_0^{\nu, b}(x)=\delta(x)$, for any choice of distribution $\nu(z)$. This means that the backwards diffusion is guaranteed to hit a Dirac-delta $\mu := \delta(x)$ at time $T=0$, and we can minimise the divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\delta_0, a_{\theta}} \| \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}\right)$ w.r.t a parametrised $a_{\theta, t}$, leading to a tractable objective. 
\begin{selfproposition}[Finite-time hitting objectives from \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}]
    The loss functions from \textcite{dai1991stochastic, vargas2023bayesian, zhang2021path} are equivalent to the KL divergence between the path measure of an SDE parametrised by a forward drift $a_{\theta, t}$ and a reverse path measure parametrised by drift $b_t = x / t$:
    \[
    D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\delta_0, a_{\theta}} \| \overleftarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\nu, b}\right)\!=\!\mathbb{E}_{X \sim \overrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}^{\mu, a_{\theta}}}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \int_0^T \!a_{\theta, t}^2\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t\!+\!\ln \!\left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \mathcal{N}\left(0, T \sigma^2\right)}{\mathrm{d} \nu}\right)\left(X_T\right)\right]\!+\!\text { const.}
    \]
    \end{selfproposition}
    \begin{proof}
        We choose $\Gamma_0=\delta_0, \gamma^{+}=0$, corresponding to a standard Brownian motion, then it follows that $\gamma^{-}=\frac{x}{t}$, $\Gamma_T=\mathcal{N}\left(0, T \sigma^2\right)$, and we obtain the above by plugging these into \Cref{eq:fbrnd}.
    \end{proof}
For further details, see \textcite{follmer2005entropy,vargas2023bayesian, huang2021schrodinger}.

\textbf{Reinforce Gradient Estimators.} As an alternative to the KL-divergence, \textcite{nusken2021solving} investigated the family of log-variance divergences\label{ntn:log_variance_divergence}
\[
D_{\operatorname{Var}}^{\mathbb{U}}\left(\mathbb{Q} \| \mathbb{P}\right)=\operatorname{Var}_{x \sim \mathbb{U}}\left(\ln \frac{\mathrm{~d} \mathbb{Q}}{\mathrm{~d} \mathbb{P}}(x)\right), \label{eq:logvar}
\]
parameterised by an auxiliary \emph{reference} path measure $\mathbb{U}$. The fact that gradients of \Cref{eq:logvar} do not have to be taken with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{P}$ (see \textcite{nusken2021solving, richter2020vargrad}) reduces the computational cost and provides additional flexibility in the choice of $\mathbb{U}$, but the gradient estimates potentially suffer from higher variance. The latter drawback is alleviated somewhat by the fact that particular choices of $\mathbb{U}$ can be linked to control variate enhanced reinforce gradient estimators \parencite{richter2020vargrad} that are particularly useful when reparameterisation is not available (such as in discrete models). We note that the same divergence has also been used as a variational inference objective in \textcite{el2012bayesian}. We can replace $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ in \Cref{eq:kl_sampling} by the log-variance divergence from \Cref{eq:logvar}, which directly leads to an objective from \textcite[Section 3.2]{nusken2021solving}.

\paragraph{Additional Connections to Methodologies.} We can unify further families of algorithms under our framework, but omit these connections in this thesis in the interest of maintaining a more focused narrative centered around generative modelling and sampling. However, we mention here that under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we can represent the popular Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) algorithm (or Sinkhorn updates) in the Optimal Transport literature \parencite{fortet1940resolution,kullback1968probability,ruschendorf1995convergence, cuturi2013sinkhorn} as an Expectation Maximisation optimisation of \Cref{eq:loss_cont_kl} (See \textcite[Proposition 3.1]{vargas2023transport}). Further, we can represent Importance-Weighted Auto-Encoders (IWAEs) \parencite{burda2015importance} and Action Matching \parencite{neklyudov2022action} under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion} (see \textcite[Equation (34)]{vargas2023transport} and \textcite[Appendix C.4]{vargas2023transport}). Finally, the discretisation of the CMCD algorithm that we will propose in the next section, corresponds under some assumptions to Diffusion Normalising Flows \parencite{zhang2021diffusion} (see \textcite[See Appendix E.1.1]{vargas2023transport}).

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Advantages of Framework 4.3 in Designing Algorithms}
    \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion} gives us complete freedom in our choices of $a_t$ and $b_t$ that can satisfy various properties of interest, and impose further structure and flexibility over the forward and reverse diffusion processes, beyond what existing algorithms allow in their viewpoint. For example, score matching and unnormalised sampling tend to use ergodic diffusions, where the diffusion is guaranteed to converge to a stationary distribution (e.g. $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$) only when $T \to \infty$. However, these algorithms make a very strong assumption that at a finite time $T$, we in fact hit the stationary distribution. This assumption can result in poor performance empirically, and can result in error bounds on sampling quality that are not very tight. It drastically reduces the flexibility of the methods that we can design, and in compute-scarse situations where we discretise our diffusions for a small number of steps, performance can be really poor. Instead, under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, $a_t$ and $b_t$ can be somewhat arbitrarily designed in order to guarantee finite-time hitting in either direction. As another example, in AIS algorithms, while algorithms are designed with the goal to converge to the target distribution, there are no guarantees that intermediate steps will satisfy the marginal distributions of each annealed distribution. We will tackle this issue under our framework in the next section.
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Controlled Monte-Carlo Diffusions}
\label{sec:controlled_monte_carlo_diffusions}

In this section, we tackle the task of unnormalised density sampling. We first turn to AIS algorithms, which have established themselves as a very high-performing baseline, and show some conceptual issues with the AIS algorithm that make its performance poor in practise. Then, using \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we design a control-based approach that we term \textit{Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusions (CMCD)}, that greatly improves theoretically and empirically over AIS and related extensions, as well as F\"ollmer drift-based methods such as DDS \parencite{vargas2023denoising} and PIS \parencite{zhang2021path}.

\subsection{Revisiting Langevin Dynamics and AIS}

As we had previously alluded to in \Cref{sec:mcmc}, Langevin dynamics from \Cref{eq:langevin_dynamics} can be readily seen as a discretisation of the forward SDE
\[
\mathrm{d}X_t = \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \hpi(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma \sqrt{2}\mathrm{~d}W_t.
\]
Therefore, in order to sample from $\hpi$ using Langevin dynamics, we simply need to simulate the forward SDE above. In practise, Langevin dynamics algorithms can struggle to produce samples that are diverse and cover the entire support of $\hpi$, as using the gradient of the log-density of $\hpi$ to iteratively guide samples from a simple Gaussian distribution to $\hpi$ can be thought of as a gradient descent procedure, and as such, can get stuck in local modes of the density.

AIS algorithms (\Cref{sec:ais}) are considered as an improvement over Langevin dynamics, where they define a series of annealed distributions from the initial distribution $\pi_0$ to the target $\hpi$ in the form\label{ntn:annealing_weights}
\[
 \pi_t \propto \pi_0^{1 - \beta_t} \hpi^{\beta_t},
\]
% NOTE: Other distributions can exist, cite MAxence Noble paper.
and then define a forward Markov process (with a corresponding joint distribution called the \emph{extended proposal distribution}) that transforms samples from $\pi_0$ to $\hpi$ iteratively, and simultaneously, a backward Markov process (with a corresponding joint distribution called the \emph{extended target distribution}) that transforms samples from $\hpi$ to $\pi_0$. Finally, the importance weights are defined as the ratio of the probability densities of the extended target distribution over the extended proposal distribution. In the continuous limit, both the forward and backward Markov processes in \Cref{eq:ais_extended_proposal} and \Cref{eq:ais_extended_target} converge to forward and backward SDEs with corresponding path measures $\fwdP{\pi_0}{a_t}$ and $\bwdP{\hpi}{b_t}$, as seen in
\[
\pi_0\left(x_0\right) \prod_{t=1}^T F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right) \rightarrow \dd X_t = a_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \dd W_t, \quad Q(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \fwdP{\pi_0}{a_t}, \\
\hpi\left(x_T\right) \prod_{t=1}^T B_{t+1}\left(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}\right) \rightarrow \dd X_t = b_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \dd \overleftarrow{W}_t, \quad \Gamma(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \bwdP{\hpi}{b_t}.
\]
We can readily see that the importance weights from \Cref{eq:ais_weights} converge to the Radon-Nikodym derivative between the forward and backward path measures. We can finally use \Cref{prop:fbrnd} to calculate the Radon-Nikodym derivative in closed-form, giving us
\[
    w_{\text{AIS}} = \frac{\Gamma(x_{0:T})}{Q(x_{0:T})} \rightarrow \frac{\dd \bwdP{\hpi}{b_t}}{\dd \fwdP{\pi_0}{a_t}}.
\]

\textbf{Unadjusted Langevin Annealing.} ULA \parencite{wu2020stochastic,welling2011bayesian,thin2021monte} is a variant of AIS that parametrises the forward process by time-dependent Langevin dynamics. That is to say, at each time step, Langevin dynamics is driven by the corresponding annealed distribution, instead of just the target distribution, giving us
\[
\dd X_t = \sigma^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln \pi_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \dd W_t. \label{eq:ula_sde}
\]
Correspondingly, ULA makes the choice of a backwards drift $b_t$ that is roughly the negative of the forward drift $a_t$ at each time step $t$, making a local assumption that at each time step $t$, the forward and backward transitions should be roughly equal, giving us the reverse SDE\footnote{Remember that a ``reverse-time'' SDE assumes we are integrating backwards in time, and as such, the sign of the drift term is negative, as $\dd t$ will be negative itself.}
\[
\dd X_t = -\sigma^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln \pi_t(X_t) \dd t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \dd \overleftarrow{W}_t. \label{eq:ula_reverse_sde}
\]
Then, we can easily write down the importance weights for ULA as\footnote{We suppress the explicit dependence on $t$ when writing the RND, as well as explicit dependences on $X_t$ when it is clear from context.}
\[
w_{\text{ULA}} = \frac{\dd \bwdP{\hpi}{- \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi}}{\dd \fwdP{\pi_0}{\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi}}.
\]
Ideally, in order to guarantee that the evolving sample $X_t$ actually has the correct law of the annealed density $\pi_t$ whose gradient log-density is being used, Langevin dynamics would need to be simulated for a large number of steps at each time step $t$, making the cost of generating samples from ULA prohibitively expensive. Instead, it is quite common in practise to simply simulate the forward SDE in \Cref{eq:ula_sde} for one step per annealed distribution. Due to this, ULA in practise needs a large number of time steps $t$ when discretised, and there are no guarantees that the marginal distribution of $X_t$ will ever satisfy the law of the annealed distributions $\pi_t$. In fact, there are no guarantees that the generated sample will even be close to the target distribution $\hpi$, unless the number of steps $t$ is taken to be very large.

\textbf{Monte Carlo Diffusions.} (MCD) \parencite{doucet2022score} point out that the extended target distribution used in AIS is suboptimal, as it does not actually correspond to the reversal of the extended proposal distribution. In fact, as shown in \textcite{del2006sequential}, the optimal backward process (corresponding to the extended target distribution) that minimises the variance of the importance weights is given by the time-reversal of the forward process, which is a quantity that we know in closed form from \Cref{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes}. Therefore, MCD proposes to use the time-reversed forward process as the optimal backward process, giving us a choice of $a_t = \sigma^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln \pi_t(X_t)$ (as before) and $b_t = \sigma^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln \pi_t(X_t) - 2 \sigma^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln q_t(X_t)$, where $q_t$ is the correct marginal proposal distribution from \Cref{eq:ais_marginal_proposal}. Unfortunately, the marginal proposal distribution $q_t$ is typically unknown, and MCD proposes a score-matching based loss function to learn a parametrised approximation $s_{\theta, t}$ to $q_t$. Under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we can readily show that the loss function from MCD is equivalent to minimising the KL-divergence between the forward and backward path measures, giving us
\[
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{MCD}}(s_{\theta, t}) = D_{\text{KL}}(\fwdP{\pi_0}{\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi} \| \bwdP{\hpi}{\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi - 2 \sigma^2 s_{\theta}}).
\]
However, similar to ULA, we continue to face the issue that simulating the forward SDE in \Cref{eq:ula_sde} for a large number of steps is prohibitively expensive, and there are no guarantees that the marginal distribution of $X_t$ will ever satisfy the law of the annealed distributions $\pi_t$. In fact, there are no guarantees that the generated sample will even be close to the target distribution $\hpi$, unless the number of steps $t$ is taken to be very large. Furthermore, while the reverse process is now accurately modelled as the correct time-reversal, it too is not guaranteed to satisfy the law of the annealed distributions $\pi_t$ at any time step $t$ (it is guaranteed to satisfy the marginal distributions $q_t$, which are different from the annealed distributions), or even converge to the initial distribution $\pi_0$ unless at large $t$.

\subsection{Learning Forward and Backward Transitions Simultaneously}

In order to address the issues with ULA and MCD, we propose a new algorithm that we term \textit{Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusions (CMCD)}. This algorithm learns both the forward and backward transitions simultaneously, and as such, is able to solve both issues that plague ULA and MCD. Firstly, we learn a forward and backward process with an additional \emph{control} term, through which we are able to actively guide the processes towards the terminal distribution at a finite time (i.e. $T=0$ and $T=T$) instead of only asymptotically. Secondly, the control term allows us to further ensure that we can actively guide the forward and backward processes such that the marginal distribution of $X_t$ will satisfy the law of the annealed distributions $\pi_t$ at each time $t$.

Under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, we have discussed how complete flexibility in $a$ and $b$ will render the minima of $D(\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}\|\ola{\P}^{\nu,b})$ highly nonunique. Furthermore, the approaches surveyed at the end of the previous section circumvent this problem by fixing either $\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}$ or $\ola{\P}^{\nu,b}$. However, to leverage the full power of diffusion models, both $\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}$ or $\ola{\P}^{\nu,b}$ should be adapted to the problem at hand. The CMCD algorithm is a method that learns both the forward and backward transitions simultaneously, by imposing additional parametrised constraints on $a$ and $b$. 
% We end this section by presenting our new CMCD sampler connecting it to prior methodology within VI \parencite{doucet2022score,geffner2023langevin,papamakarios2017masked} and OT where we can view CMCD as an instance of entropy regularised OT in the infinite constraint limit \parencite{bernton2019schr}.

First, we fix a prescribed curve of distributions $(\pi_t)_{t \in [0,T]}$, whose scores $\nabla \ln \pi_t$ (and unnormalised densities $\hat{\pi}_t$) are assumed to be available in tractable form; this is the scenario typically encountered in AIS and related approaches towards computing posterior expectations \parencite{neal2001annealed,reich2011dynamical,heng2015gibbs,heng2020controlled,arbel2021annealed,doucet2022score}. The \emph{Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusion sampler} (CMCD) then learns a vector field $\nabla \phi_t$ as an additional control term in the forward annealed Langevin dynamics, giving us
\[
\label{eq:controlled annealing} 
\d X_t  \!=\! \left(\sigma^2\nabla \ln \pi_t(X_t)\! +\! \nabla \phi_t (X_t)\right) \d t + \sigma \!\sqrt{2 } \,\d W_t, \qquad X_0 \!\sim \!\pi_0, 
\]
such that \Cref{eq:controlled annealing} has the annealed distributions as the marginal distribution and $\pi_T$ as the terminal distribution, i.e., $\ora{\P}^{\pi_0,\sigma^2\nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi}_t = \pi_t$, for all $t \in [0,T]$\footnote{Note that if $\pi_t$ were constant in time ($\pi_t = \pi_0$), then $\phi = 0$ would reduce \Cref{eq:controlled annealing} to equilibrium Langevin dynamics, preserving $\pi_0$.}. With $\pi_t$ varying in time, $\nabla \phi_t$ can be thought of as a \emph{control} that enables transitions between neighbouring densities $\pi_t$ and $\pi_{t + \delta t}$ such that \Cref{eq:controlled annealing} has the correct marginal and terminal distributions.\label{ntn:control_term}

To learn the parameters for $\nabla \phi_t$ we invoke \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}, but restrict $\ola{\P}^{\pi_T,b}$ to retain uniqueness. Specifically, we use \Cref{prop:time_reversal_of_sdes} to motivate our choice of $b_t$ as the exact time-reversal of \Cref{eq:controlled annealing}, giving us\footnote{We note that the additional factor of $2$ in Nelson's relation is due to the noise scaling $\sigma\sqrt{2} \d W_t$ in \Cref{eq:controlled annealing}.}: 
\[b_t := (\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t + \nabla \phi_t) - 2 \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t = - \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t + \nabla \phi_t.
\]
Therefore, the loss function for CMCD is given by
\[
\label{eq:annealed_div}
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_D(\phi) := D \left( {\ora{\P}^{\pi_0,\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi}} \| {\ola{\P}^{\pi_T, - \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi}} \right), 
\]
which is valid for any choice of divergence $D$. The constraint $b_t = a_t - 2\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t$ ensures uniqueness under \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion}:
\begin{selfproposition}[Existence and uniqueness of CMCD]
    \label{prop:annealing uniqueness}
    Under mild conditions on $(\pi_t)_{t \in [0,T]}$, \Cref{eq:annealed_div} admits a ($\pi_t$-a.e.) unique minimiser $\phi^*$, up to additive constants, satisfying $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CMCD}}(\phi^*) = 0$. 
\end{selfproposition}
\begin{proof}
    See Appendix \ref{app:proof_uniqueness_cmcd}. % NOTE: Add the proof of uniqueness here.
\end{proof}

We therefore define the CMCD sampler as :

\begin{selfdefinition}[Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusion Sampler (CMCD)]
    Given the forward and backward SDEs
    \begin{subequations}
        \[
            \d X_t &= \left(\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t(X_t) + \nabla \phi_t(X_t)\right) \d t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \d W_t, \qquad X_0 \sim \pi_0, \\
            \d X_t &= \left(- \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t(X_t) + \nabla \phi_t(X_t)\right) \d t + \sigma \sqrt{2} \d \ola{W}_t, \qquad X_T \sim \pi_T.
    \]
    \end{subequations}
    We define the corresponding forward and backward path measures as
        \[
            \ora{\P}^{\pi_0, \phi}_{\text{CMCD}} := \ora{\P}^{\pi_0, \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi} \quad \text{and} \quad \ola{\P}^{\pi_T, \phi}_{\text{CMCD}} := \ola{\P}^{\pi_T, - \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi}.
        \]
    The optimal vector field $\phi^{\star}$ is calculated as the unique minimiser (see \Cref{prop:annealing uniqueness}) of the following loss function\label{ntn:optimal_control_term}
    \[
        \phi^{*} = \argmin_{\phi} \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_D(\phi) = \argmin_{\phi} D \left( \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0} \| \bwdPcmcd{\pi_T} \right), \label{eq:cmcd_divergence_loss}
    \]
    Then, the importance weights for CMCD are defined as the RND between the backward and forward path measures, giving us
    \begin{subequations}
        \label{eq:cmcd_fbrnd}
    \[
        \ln w_{\text{CMCD}} &= \ln \left(\frac{\d \bwdPcmcdstar{\pi_T}}{\d \fwdPcmcdstar{\pi_0}} \right), \\
        &= \ln \pi_0(X_0) - \ln \pi_T(X_T) \\
        & +\!\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \!\int_0^T \!\left(\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t\!+\!\nabla \phi^{\star}_t\right)\left(X_t\right) \!\cdot \!\mathrm{d} X_t\!-\!\frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \!\int_0^T\! \!\|\!\left(\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t\!+\!\nabla \phi^{\star}_t\right)\!\left(X_t\right)\!\|^2 \!\mathrm{~d} t \\
        & -\!\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \!\int_0^T \!\left(\nabla \phi^{\star}_t\!-\!\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t\right)\left(X_t\right)\! \cdot \!\mathrm{d} \ola{X}_t\!+\!\frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} \!\int_0^T\!\!\|\!\left(\nabla \phi^{\star}_t\!-\!\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t\right)\!\left(X_t\right)\!\|^2 \!\mathrm{~d} t .
    \]
    \end{subequations}
\end{selfdefinition}
\subsection{Expectations and Normalising Constant Estimation with CMCD}

Given the optimal vector field $\nabla \phi_t^*$, we are now able to accomplish the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We can produce samples from the unnormalised probability distribution $\hpi_T$ by simulating \Cref{eq:controlled annealing}. We write this in notation as $X_{0:T} \sim \ora{\P}^{\pi_0,\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \phi^*}$, where $X_T$ is a sample from $\hpi_T$.
    \item We can take expectations of any function $f$ with respect to an unnormalised density $\hpi_T$ by simulating \Cref{eq:controlled annealing} and taking the expectation with respect to the sample paths. We can compare directly to \Cref{eq:is_expectation_estimate} to obtain an unbiased estimator of the expectation, giving us
    \[
        \mathbb{E}_{\pi_T}[f] = \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[ f(X_T) \left( \frac{\d \bwdPcmcd{\hpi_T}}{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \right)(X_{0:T}) \right] / \hat{Z},
    \]
    where we assume that the backwards SDE has the unnormalised $\hpi_T$ as the initial distribution, and not $\pi_T$. Therefore, we define the expectation under this unnormalised FBRND by an estimate of the normalising constant, which we calculate below.
    \item We can estimate $Z$ in $\pi_T = \hat{\pi}_T/Z$ unbiasedly via
    \[
    \hat{Z} = \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[\frac{\d \bwdPcmcd{\hpi_T}}{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} (X_{0:T}) \right], \label{eq:normalising_cmcd}
    \]
    and is valid for any (possibly suboptimal) $\nabla \phi_t$.
\end{enumerate}
We have an important identity that shows that at optimality, CMCD results in a zero-variance estimator of the normalising constant:
\begin{selfproposition}[CMCD has Optimal Zero-Variance Normalising Constant Estimator]
    \label{prop:zero_variance_normalising_constant}
    The estimator of $Z$ given by \Cref{eq:normalising_cmcd}, at optimality of $\nabla \phi^*_t$ yields a zero-variance estimator. That is:
    \[
    Z = \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[\frac{\d \bwdPcmcd{\hpi_T}}{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \right] = \frac{\d \bwdPcmcdstar{\hpi_T}}{\d \fwdPcmcdstar{\pi_0}} (X_{0:T}),
    \]
    where the right-hand side shows that optimality of $\nabla \phi^{\star}$ yields a zero-variance estimator for $Z$, as the statement holds almost surely in $X_{0:T}$, without taking the expectation.
    % 
\end{selfproposition}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix \ref{app:proof_zero_variance_normalising_constant}, where we first show a unique connection between our normalising constant estimator and Crooks' fluctuation theorem, as well as Jaszynski's equality.
\end{proof}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: On Samples from Path Measures}
    In these sections, we are taking expectations of functions under the forward and backward path measures. Expectations are taken over ``sample paths'' from the forward path measure as $X_{0:T} \sim \ora{\P}^{\mu, a}$, where we first sample $X_0 \sim \mu$ and then simulate the SDE forward to obtain intermediate $X_{1:T}$ (in discrete-time, we could use the Euler-Maruyama method from \Cref{def:euler-maruyama-method}). Similarly, a ``sample path'' from the backward path measure is defined as $X_{T:0} \sim \ola{\P}^{\nu, b}$, where we first sample $X_T \sim \nu$ and then simulate the SDE backward to obtain intermediate $X_{1:T}$. 
\end{lightgraybox}

\subsection{Discretisation of the Continuous-Time Limit}

In this section, we make specific choices for the divergence $D$ in order to derive two versions of CMCD. Further, we derive a discretisation to our continuous-time RND which is used in practise whenever we need to train and simulate our algorithm computationally. Firstly, we define a discretisation of our RND with a specific choice of reference measure, which greatly simplifies the terms involved:

\begin{selfproposition}[Discretisation of the FBRND]
    \label{prop:fbrnd_euler}
    Letting $\Gamma_0=\Gamma_T=\mathrm{Leb}$ and $\gamma^{\pm} = 0$, we have that the RND in \Cref{eq:fbrnd} is given by 
    \begin{subequations}
    \begin{align*}
    \ln \left(\frac{\d\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}}{\d\ola{\P}^{\nu,b}}\right)(X) = \ln\mu(X_0) - \ln \nu(X_T) &+ \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\int_0^T a_t(X_t) \cdot  \d X_t - \tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2} \int_0^T|| a_t(X_t)||^2 \, \d t \\
    &-  \tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\int_0^T b_t (X_t) \cdot \d \ola{X}_t  +\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\int_0^T ||b_t (X_t)|||^2 \, \d t,
    \end{align*}
    \end{subequations}
    and admits the following discrete-time approximation up to constant terms in $a_t$ and $b_t$ (following \Cref{rem:conversion}),
    \begin{align}
        \ln \left( \widehat{\frac{\d\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}}{\d\ola{\P}^{\nu,b}}}\right)(X) &= \ln \mu(X_0) - \ln \nu(X_T) \nonumber \\
        &+ \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} \tfrac{1}{2 \sigma^2 (t_{i+1} - t_{i})}  \| X_{t_{i}}  - X_{t_{i+1}} + b_{t_{i+1}}(X_{t_{i+1}}) (t_{i+1} - t_{i})\|^2 \nonumber \\
        &- \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} \tfrac{1}{2 \sigma^2 (t_{i+1} - t_{i})}  \| X_{t_{i+1}}  - X_{t_{i}} - a_{t_{i}}(X_{t_{i}}) (t_{i+1} - t_{i})||^2. \label{eq:fbrnd_euler_discrete}
    \end{align}
    Equivalently, writing $\delta = t_{i+1} - t_i$, we have that
    \begin{align}
        \ln \left( \widehat{\frac{\d\ora{\P}^{\mu,a}}{\d\ola{\P}^{\nu,b}}}\right)(X) &= \ln \left[\frac{\mu(X_0)}{\nu(X_T)} \prod_{i=0}^{K-1} \frac{\mathcal{N}(X_{t_{i+1}} \mid X_{t_{i}} + a_{t_{i}}(X_{t_{i}}) \delta, 2 \sigma^2 \delta)}{\mathcal{N}(X_{t_{i}} \mid X_{t_{i+1}} - b_{t_{i+1}}(X_{t_{i+1}}) \delta, 2 \sigma^2 \delta)} \right]. \label{eq:fbrnd_euler_discrete_2}
    \end{align}
    \end{selfproposition}
    \begin{proof}
        See Appendix \ref{app:proof_fbrnd_euler}.
    \end{proof}    

    Now, we can use \Cref{prop:fbrnd} and \Cref{prop:fbrnd_euler} to derive a loss function for CMCD using the KL divergence.

    \begin{selfdefinition}[KL Divergence Loss for CMCD]
        \label{def:cmcd_kl_loss}
        Plugging in the KL divergence in \Cref{eq:cmcd_divergence_loss} and using \Cref{prop:fbrnd}, we obtain the loss function
        \[
            \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\phi) &= \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}}{\d \bwdPcmcd{\pi_T}} \right) \right]. \\
            &=\mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[\sigma^2 \int_0^T\left|\nabla \ln \pi_t\left(X_t\right)\right|^2 \mathrm{~d} t \right. \nonumber \\
            & \quad \left.+\frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2}} \int_0^T\left(\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_t-\nabla \phi_t\right)\left(X_t\right) \cdot \mathrm{d} \overrightarrow{W}_t-\ln \hat{\pi}_T\left(X_T\right)\right]+\text {const.} \label{eq:cmcd_kl_loss_continuous}
        \]
        Upon discretisation of the time interval $[0, T]$ into $K$ subintervals such that $(t_0 = 0, t_1, \dots, t_K = T)$ and $\Delta t_k = (t_{k+1} - t_k)$, the discretisation of the loss function is given by
        \[
            \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_{\mathrm{KL}}\!(\phi) \!= \!\E\!\left[\ln\! \frac{{\pi}_0(X_0)}{\hat{\pi}(X_T)} \!\prod_{k=0}^{K-1} \!\frac{\mathcal{N}(X_{t_{k+1}} | X_{t_{k}} + (\sigma^2\nabla \ln \pi_{t_k} + \nabla \phi_{t_k}) (X_{t_{k}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k )}{\mathcal{N}\!(X_{t_{k}} | X_{t_{k+1}}\!\! \!+ \!(\sigma^2 \nabla\! \ln \pi_{t_{k+1}}\! \!- \!\!\nabla\! \phi_{t_{k+1}})(X_{t_{k+1}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k )}\!\right].\!\! \label{eq:cmcd_kl_loss}
        \]
    \end{selfdefinition}
    \begin{proof}
    See Appendix \ref{app:proof_cmcd_kl_loss_continuous}.
    \end{proof}
    From \Cref{eq:cmcd_fbrnd}, we can see that there is a direct relationship between the importance weights and the term within the expectation in \Cref{eq:cmcd_kl_loss}, giving us
    \[
        \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}}{\d \bwdPcmcd{\pi_T}} \right) \right] = \mathbb{E}_{X_{0:T} \sim \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}} \left[ - \ln W_{\text{CMCD}} \right].
    \]
    Therefore, working with the discretised KL divergence loss in \Cref{eq:cmcd_kl_loss}, we define \Cref{alg:kl_cmcd} for training, sampling and estimating the normalising constant.
    \begin{algorithm}[t]  
        \caption{ CMCD - Training, Sampling and Normalizing Constant Estimation} 
        \label{alg:kl_cmcd}
        \begin{algorithmic}
        \Require $\pi_0$, $\pi_T$, $\pi_t$, $\sigma$, $K$ step-sizes $\Delta t_{k}$,  neural network $f^{\phi}$.
        \For{$i$ in $\mathrm{epochs}$}
        \State $X_0 \sim \pi_0$
        \State $ \ln W_{\text{CMCD}} = -\ln \pi_0(X_0)$
        \For{$k=0$ to $K-1$}
        \State $X_{t_{k+1}} \sim \mathcal{N}\Big(X_{t_{k+1}} \big| X_{t_{k}} + (\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_{t_k} +f^{\phi}_{t_k}) (X_{t_{k}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k \Big)$
        \State $\ln W_{\text{CMCD}}  = \ln W_{\text{CMCD}} + \ln \frac{ \mathcal{N}\Big(X_{t_{k}} \big| X_{t_{k+1}} + (\sigma^2\nabla  \ln \pi_{t_{k+1}} - f^{\phi}_{t_{k+1}})(X_{t_{k+1}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k \Big)}{\mathcal{N}\Big(X_{t_{k+1}} \big| X_{t_{k}} + (\sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi_{t_k} +f^{\phi}_{t_k}) (X_{t_{k}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k \Big)}$
        \EndFor
        \State $\ln W_{\text{CMCD}}  = \ln W_{\text{CMCD}} + \ln \pi_T(X_{T})$
        \State $\text{Take Gradient Step } \nabla_{\phi} (- \ln W_{\text{CMCD}})$
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\ln W_{\text{CMCD}}, \text{Estimate of } \ln Z\approx \ln W_{\text{CMCD}} ,  \text{Approximate sample } X_{T}$
        \end{algorithmic}
    \end{algorithm}

    We note that the first line in \Cref{eq:cmcd_kl_loss_continuous} is akin to the optimal control type objectives of F{\"o}llmer and DDS samplers recalled at the end of \Cref{sec:unnormalised_density_sampling_cmcd} \parencite{berner2022optimal}. Furthermore, note that setting $\phi = 0$ in \Cref{eq:cmcd_kl_loss} recovers Unadjusted Langevin Annealing (ULA), see, \textcite[Eq. 14]{thin2021monte} or \textcite[Eq. 21]{geffner2023langevin}; hence, we can view CMCD as a controlled version of ULA. Setting $\phi = 0$ \emph{only in the numerator} is akin to Monte Carlo Diffusion (MCD), see Algorithm 1 and Eq. 34 in \textcite{doucet2022score}.   Finally, action matching \parencite{neklyudov2022action} can be recovered from $D=\text{KL}$ and \Cref{framework:unifying_framework_diffusion} by choosing the reference $\ora{\P}^{\Gamma_0,\gamma^+} = \ola{\P}^{\Gamma_T,\gamma^-}$ in \Cref{prop:fbrnd} appropriately.

    \begin{selfdefinition}[Log-Variance Loss for CMCD]
        \label{def:cmcd_var_loss}
        Plugging in the Log-Variance divergence in \Cref{eq:cmcd_divergence_loss} and using \Cref{prop:fbrnd}, we obtain the loss function
        \[
            \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_{\mathrm{Var}}(\phi) = \mathbb{V}_{X_{0:T} \sim \mathbb{U}} \left[ \ln \left( \frac{\d \fwdPcmcd{\pi_0}}{\d \bwdPcmcd{\pi_T}} \right) \right],
        \]
        where $\mathbb{U}$ is any auxiliary measure that we can take samples from. In practice, we make the choice $\mathbb{U} = \ora{\P}^{\pi_0, \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \operatorname{detach}(\phi)}$, which is a detached version of the forward process, meaning that we do not backpropagate through the samples. Plugging in the FBRND from \Cref{eq:fbrnd}, and discretising the time interval $[0, T]$ into $K$ subintervals such that $(t_0 = 0, t_1, \dots, t_K = T)$ and $\Delta t_k = (t_{k+1} - t_k)$, the discretised loss function is given by
        \[
            \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CMCD}}_{\mathrm{Var}}\!(\phi)\! \approx \!\mathbb{V} \!\!\left[\ln\! \frac{{\pi}_0(X_0)}{\hat{\pi}(X_T)} \!\prod_{k=0}^{K-1} \!\frac{\mathcal{N}(X_{t_{k+1}} | X_{t_{k}} + (\sigma^2\nabla \ln \pi_{t_k} + \nabla \phi_{t_k}) (X_{t_{k}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k )}{\mathcal{N}\!(X_{t_{k}} | X_{t_{k+1}}\!\! \!+ \!(\sigma^2 \nabla\! \ln \pi_{t_{k+1}}\! \!- \!\nabla\! \phi_{t_{k+1}})(X_{t_{k+1}})\Delta t_k, 2 \sigma^2 \Delta t_k )}\!\right].\!\! \label{eq:cmcd_var_loss_discrete}
        \]
    \end{selfdefinition}

    Here, the variance is taken with respect to samples from $\mathbb{U} = \ora{\P}^{\pi_0, \sigma^2 \nabla \ln \pi + \nabla \operatorname{detach}(\phi)}$. We notice that the loss function simply swaps an expectation with a variance measurement, and in practise is quite easy to implement.

    We also derive an underdamped Langevin dynamics generalisation of CMCD, which extends the class of AIS algorithms that perform underdamped Langevin dynamics with an additional momentum variable \parencite{geffner2023langevin}, in Appendix \ref{sec:underdamped}.

We first replicate the benchmarks from \textcite{geffner2023langevin} on 6 standard target benchmark distributions. We compare against two standard baselines, Unadjusted Langevin Annealing (ULA) \parencite{wu2020stochastic, thin2021monte} and Monte Carlo Diffusion (MCD) \parencite{doucet2022annealed}; and two underdamped baselines, Uncorrected Hamiltonian Annealing (UHA) \parencite{geffner2021mcmc, ZhangAIS2021} and Langevin Diffusion Variational Inference (LDVI) \parencite{geffner2023langevin}. Furthermore, we include comparisons of $\ln Z$ estimation on two datasets with known normalising constant, the \texttt{funnel} and \texttt{gmm}, and compare against PIS \parencite{barr2020quantum, vargas2021bayesian, zhang2021path}; DDS \parencite{vargas2023denoising}; and Sequential Monte Carlo Sampler (SMC) \parencite{del2006sequential, zhou2016toward}.

We see that both overdamped and underdamped CMCD consistently outperform all baseline methods, especially at low $K$,  and in fact, across most targets even the standard CMCD algorithm outperforms the overdamped baselines. \section{Discussion}

Overall we have successfully introduced a novel variational framework bridging VI and transport using modern advances in diffusion models and processes. In particular, we have shown that many existing diffusion-based methods for generative modelling and sampling can be viewed as special instances of our proposed framework. Building on this, we have developed novel objectives for dynamic entropy regularised transports (based on a relationship between the EM and IPF algorithms) and annealed flows (with connections to fluctuation theorems due to Crook and Jarzynski, rooted in statistical physics). Finally, we have explored the CMCD inference scheme obtaining state-of-the-art results across a suite of challenging inference benchmarks. We believe this experimental success is partly due to our approach striking a balance between parametrising a flexible family of distributions whilst being constrained enough such that learning the sampler is not overly expensive \parencite{tzen2019theoretical,vargas2023expressiveness}.

Throughout this chapter, we have seen the benefits of the continuous-time framework in providing a more principled and theoretically grounded framework for developing sampling algorithms. We have seen how the continuous-time framework allows us to reason about boundary conditions and obtain finite-time guarantees of converging to source and target distributions, while also satisfying the intermediate annealing distributions, ensuring both that the sampler is guided between the source and target distributions through a carefully designed annealing schedule, and also that we preserve good trajectories when discretising these continuous processes. As we can see from \Cref{fig:cmcd_exps}, even with discretisations as low as $K = 8$, we greatly outperform other algorithms in terms of sample quality and ability to estimate the normalising constant of the target distribution. While previously, we often had either ergodic guarantees (at $T \to \infty$) or finite-time guarantees in one direction (such as DDS and PIS), our continuous-time framework allows us to obtain finite-time guarantees in both directions. 

Finally, as we discussed in the remark about the advantages of our framework, since we have complete freedom in our choice of parametrisation of both the forward and backward processes, we can be flexible in imposing regularisation and structure on the trajectories that the samples take. Unlike standard techniques such as ULA and MCD, which cannot provide any guarantees on intermediate marginal distributions satisfying the annealing schedule, our specific choice of \textit{controlled} processes allows us to ensure this, which is more accurate under discretisation, as evidenced by the results in \Cref{fig:cmcd_exps}. Since we can ``correct'' the trajectory of the samples at each intermediate time step, the CMCD algorithm maintains its quality of sampling regardless of the level of discretisation. Future directions of improving this algorithmic framework further could explore more optimal (or learnable) schemes for the annealed distributions $\pi_t$ \parencite{goshtasbpour2023adaptive} and alternate divergences that might provide better behaviour (such as mode-filling for multimodal distributions) \parencite{nusken2021solving, richter2023improved, midgley2022flow}.
