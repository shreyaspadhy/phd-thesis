In the previous chapter, we discussed a large collection of sampling algorithms, and alluded to the fact that they converge to stochastic differential equations in the continuous-time limit. As we discussed in Equation~\eqref{eq:sde_intuitive}, we are interested in differential equations of the form
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d} X_t}{\mathrm{d} t} = \mu(X_t) + \sigma(X_t) \times \text{``noise''}, \label{eq:sde_intuitive_2}
\end{equation}
where the equation is akin to an ordinary differential equation (ODE) with a drift parameter $\mu$, and random noise injected into it at each time step scaled by a diffusion parameter $\sigma$. As a result, the solution to this equation is no longer deterministic, but a random variable at each time step. This means that while the explicit value of $X_t$ is not known at each time step without knowing the initial condition $X_0$ as well as the noise, we can always write down the probability distribution $\P(X_t)$ at each time step. In this chapter, we will develop the mathematical machinery required to discuss stochastic processes and SDEs. We will then briefly show how some of our implied connections from the previous chapter arise naturally from simple design choices in the SDE framework. Finally, we highlight drawbacks of the existing SDE framework, preventing the development of a unified framework, allowing us to tackle this task in the upcoming chapters.

The stochastic calculus framework is built on measure-theoretic probability, which we describe in this section. We build upon the notation and definitions introduced in \textcite{oksendal2013stochastic}, which we refer the reader to for a more thorough treatment. Instead, we aim to provide a concise summary of the most relevant concepts, along with an intuitive understanding of the concepts.

At its core, measure-theoretic probability theory is concerned with the notion of assigning a ``measure'' to subsets of a space. This notion gets more complicated in higher dimensions (or infinite dimensions), or when we consider arbitrary collections of sets, and so we aim to be as concrete as possible. Therefore, let us put a few definitions to these abstract notions.

\label{ntn:sample_space}\label{ntn:sigma-algebra}
\textbf{Probability Measure and Probability Space.} We consider the \textit{sample space} $\Omega$, which is the set of all possible outcomes $\omega$ of an experiment. When an experiment is performed, the outcome is some element of $\Omega$ based on a law of probability $\mathbb{P}$. We then consider an \textit{event} $A$ to be a subset of $\Omega$, which is a collection of some of the possible outcomes. It is useful to restrict any theoretical considerations we have to the \textit{$\sigma$-algebra} $\mathcal{F}$, which is a collection that contains $\Omega$ along with all subsets of $\Omega$ that are closed under countable unions, intersections, and complements. In general, the $\sigma$-algebra is a subset of the power set $\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$, as there are often cases when the power set can contain pathological sets that are not measurable. We define a \textit{probability measure} as the map $\P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ that satisfies the following properties:
\begin{align}
    \text{(P0)} \quad & \P(\emptyset) = 0 \\
    \text{(P1)} \quad & \P(\Omega) = 1 \\
    \text{(P2)} \quad & \P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \P(A_i) \quad \text{for all } A_i \in \mathcal{F}.
\end{align}
\label{ntn:prob}
\label{ntn:probability-measure}
The triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \P)$ is called a \textit{probability space}. Further, we say that an event $A \in \mathcal{F}$ happens \textit{almost surely (a.s.)} if $\P(A) = 1$\footnote{Note that this is not the same as saying that $A = \Omega$, as there may be elements in $\Omega$ that are not in $A$, but this set of elements has measure zero.}.

\label{ntn:borel-sigma-algebra}\label{ntn:lebesgue-measure}
\textbf{Borel $\sigma$-algebra and Lebesgue Measure.} In the special case when we consider $\Omega$ to be the real space $\mathbb{R}^n$ (which is one example of a topological space), we further restrict our attention to the Borel $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, which is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra that contains all open sets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Correspondingly, we can define the "canonical" measure on $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, known as the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$, which assigns a measure to each Borel set.

\textbf{Random Variables.} Probability spaces give us a way to assign probabilities to collections of interesting subsets of $\Omega$. However, we don't often work with the sets themselves, which can be hard to visualise or enumerate, but functions that map these sets (or events) to the real line. We define a $\mathcal{F}$-\textit{measurable function} $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}^n$ to be a function that satisfies $X^{-1}(B) \in \mathcal{F}$ for all $B \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$. We also call such functions \textit{random variables}\footnote{A somewhat unfortunate choice of terminology, as random variables are not variables at all, but rather functions that map the sample space to the real line.}.

\textbf{Change of Measure and the Radon-Nikodym Theorem.} On a given measurable space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$, if we consider two measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ such that they are \textit{absolutely continuous} with respect to each other (i.e. $\nu \ll \mu$), we can express $\nu$ in terms of $\mu$ using a change of measure. Intuitively, we could think of this as a process of assigning a ``weight'' to each element of $\Omega$ such that when we take the weighted sum of elements over a measurable subset $A \in \mathcal{F}$ with measure $\mu(A)$, we get the measure of $A$ under $\nu$. That is, there exists a function $f: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $\nu(A) = \int_A f d\mu$. This function $f$ is known as the \textit{Radon-Nikodym derivative} of $\nu$ with respect to $\mu$, and is denoted by $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$.

\textbf{Pushforward Measure.} Considering two measurable spaces $(\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2)$, if we have a measurable function $f: \Omega_1 \to \Omega_2$ and a measure $\mu$ on $\Omega_1$, we can define a new measure $\nu$ on $\Omega_2$ by $\nu(A) = \mu(f^{-1}(A)), \forall A \in \mathcal{F}_2$. This measure $\nu$ is known as the \textit{pushforward measure} of $\mu$ by $f$, and is denoted by $f_\ast \mu$. We can think of this as a process of ``pushing'' the measure $\mu$ through the function $f$ to define a new measure on $\Omega_2$.

\textbf{Probability Distributions and Probability Density Functions.} Since random variables are functions that map the space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \P)$ to the real line $(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n))$, there exists a pushforward measure of $\P$ through $X$, which we write as $X_\ast \P$. This pushforward measure is termed the \textit{probability distribution} of $X$, and is denoted by $\P_X$. Since $\P_X$ is a measure defined on $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can further define the Radon-Nikodym derivative of $\P_X$ with respect to the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is known as the \textit{probability density function} of $X$, and is denoted by $f_X$.

\label{ntn:stochastic-process}
\begin{definition}[Stochastic Process]
    A \textit{stochastic process} $\{X_t\}_{t \in T}$ is a parametric collection of random variables indexed by a parameter $t \in T$, which we call the \textit{index set}. 
\end{definition}

While we can define stochastic processes on arbitrary index sets, we are particularly interested in the case when $T$ is the time index $t \in [0, \infty)$. Later on, when we discuss Gaussian processes, we will see that the index set can also be a continuous domain, such as the real line. 

\begin{definition}[Filtration and Filtered Probability Space]
    A filtration of the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is a sequence of sigma algebras $\left\{\mathcal{F}_t\right\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ satisfying $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ if $s \leq t$.
    We call a filtered probability space the tuple $\left(\Omega, \mathcal{F},\left\{\mathcal{F}_t\right\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}, \mathbb{P}\right)$.
\end{definition}

Intuitively, a filtration is a way of representing the information available at each time $t$. We can study the properties of the stochastic process $X_t$ at a specific time $t$ by working within the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_t$, which represents the information available up to that time. As time passes, more information becomes available, and so the $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}_t$ becomes larger as $t$ increases.

\begin{definition}[Adapted and Predictable Processes]
    Let $\left(\Omega, \mathcal{F},\left\{\mathcal{F}_t\right\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}, \mathbb{P}\right)$ be a filtered probability space. A stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted if $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable, and is $\mathcal{F}_t$-predictable if $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t-}$-measurable.
\end{definition}

We can also generate a new filtered probability space given a stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$, by considering the filtration generated by the process, which is the smallest filtration that makes the process adapted.

\begin{definition}[Filtration Generated by a Process]
    Let $\{X_t\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ be a stochastic process. The filtration generated by $\left\{X_t\right\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ is defined as $\mathcal{F}_t^X=\sigma\left\{X_0, \ldots, X_t\right\}$, and the process $\left\{X_t\right\}_{t \in \mathcal{T}}$ is $\mathcal{F}_n^X$-adapted by construction.
\end{definition}

Further, we will consider a special family of stochastic processes that have very useful properties, and pop up often in our further discussions: martingales. 

\begin{definition}[Martingale]
    A stochastic process $X_t$ is a martingale with respect to a filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$ if the following properties hold:
    \begin{align}
        \text{(P1)} \quad &X_t \text{ is } \mathcal{F}_t \text{ measurable for all } t \in \mathcal{T} \text{ (it is adapted)} \\
        \text{(P2)} \quad &\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_t\right|\right]<\infty \text{ for all } t \in \mathcal{T} \text{ (it is integrable)} \label{eq:martingale_p2} \\
        \text{(P3)} \quad &\mathbb{E}\left[X_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s\right]=X_s \text{a.s. for all } s \leq t. \label{eq:martingale_p3}
    \end{align}
\end{definition}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: An Intuitive View of Martingales}
    The word (and really the concept) of a martingale originally came from game theory and gambling, where a martingale referred to a betting strategy where the present is the best approximation of the future, and on average, you would not win or lose money, but stay the same. In the context of stochastic processes, a martingale is a stochastic process that is a "fair" process, in the sense that it neither grows nor decays on average. This is quite easily seen through \Cref{eq:martingale_p3}, where the expected value of the process at time $t$, given only information up to time $s$, is exactly the value of the process at time $s$.
\end{lightgraybox}

Another useful property of a martingale for us to remember for later is that it is a stochastic process where the optimal regressor of a future value conditioned on the past is the past value itself. We first define the optimal predictor of mean-squared error

\begin{theorem}[Optimal Predictor of Mean-Squared Error]
    Let $X, Y$ be $\mathscr{L}^2$ random variables and
    \[
    f^*=\argmin_{f-\text {measurable}} \mathbb{E}_{X, Y}\left[\|f(X)-Y\|^2\right]
    .
    \]
    Then it follows that $f^*(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X=x]$.
\end{theorem}

Applying the optimal predictor of a mean-squared error theorem to the martingale, gives us the following corollary
\begin{corollary}[Martingale as Optimal Predictor]
    \[
    f^*=\argmin_{f-\text {measurable}} \mathbb{E}[ \|f(X_t)-X_{t+\Delta t}\|^2], \quad f^*(X_t)=\mathbb{E}[X_{t+\Delta t} \mid X_t]=X_t
    .
    \]
\end{corollary}

\section{A Discrete-Time Intuition for SDEs}

Now that we have introduced the necessary mathematical framework, let us revisit the SDE in Equation~\eqref{eq:sde_intuitive_2}. The main challenge in this differential equation is the inability to concretely parameterise the noise term. To address this, let us consider a discrete-time approximation of this equation with a simple choice of $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$. If we discretise the time interval $[0, 1]$ into $N$ intervals of size $\Delta t = \frac{1}{N}$, we can approximate the solution to the SDE at time $t=1$ as
\begin{equation}
    X_{1} = X_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} Z_i,
\end{equation}
where $Z_i$ refers to the noise term at each time step. We need to decide a few properties of the noise term $Z_i$. First, we assume that the noise terms are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). Second, we need to decide the distribution of the noise terms. However, anytime we are faced with a sum over i.i.d. random variables, we can rely on the Central Limit Theorem to tell us that the distribution of the sum will be Gaussian. If we make some reasonable assumptions about the distribution of $Z_i$, i.e. specify the first and second moments as $\E[Z_i] = \nu N^{-1}$ and $\mathbb{V}[Z_i] = \zeta^2 N^{-1}$, we have
\begin{equation}
    X_{1} - X_0 \approx \mathcal{N}\left(\nu, \zeta^2\right).
\end{equation}
We extend this result to a general time $t$ by dividing each unit interval into $N$ subintervals, giving us the difference between any $X_t$ and $X_s$ (for $s \leq t$) as
\begin{equation}
    X_t - X_s \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\nu (t-s), \zeta^2 (t-s)\right). \label{eq:discrete_diff}
\end{equation}

\begin{definition}[Discrete-Time Noise Process]
    \label{def:discrete_time_noise_process}
    We define the discrete-time noise process $\Xt$ as the collection of random variables that satisfies the following properties:
    \1 Normally-distributed increments: $X_t-X_s \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\nu(t-s), \zeta^2(t-s)\right)$.
    \2 Independent increments: $X_t-X_s$ is independent of $X_u-X_v$ for any $s, t, u, v$ such that $0 \leq s<t \leq u<v$.
    \0
\end{definition}

Our discrete-time approximation of the SDE shows us that the noise term has these two properties. In order to extend this result to the continuous-time limit, where we can say that any collection of random variables $\Xt$ has these properties over the continuous domain of time, we use the following theorem:

\begin{theorem}[Kolmogorov's Continuity Criterion]
    \label{thm:kolmogorov_continuity_criterion}
    If $\Xt$ is a real-valued process such that for all $T>0$ there exist constants $\alpha, \beta, \gamma>0$ with
    \[
    \mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{t+h}-X_t\right|^\alpha\right] \leq \gamma h^{1+\beta}
    .
    \]
    for every $t$ and $0 \leq h \leq T$, then there exists a modification of $X_t$ which has almost-surely continuous paths.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
 See \textcite[Pg. 51]{stroock1997multidimensional}.
\end{proof}

Using \Cref{thm:kolmogorov_continuity_criterion}, we can now extend our discrete-time approximation of the noise term in \Cref{def:discrete_time_noise_process} to the continuous-time limit.
\begin{proposition}[Continuous Modification of Noise]
    \label{prop:continuous_modification_of_noise}
    There exists a modification of the stochastic process $\Xt$ defined in \Cref{def:discrete_time_noise_process} that has almost-surely continuous paths.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    We can use \Cref{thm:kolmogorov_continuity_criterion} to show that there exists a modification of the stochastic process $\Xt$ that has almost-surely continuous paths. From \Cref{eq:discrete_diff}, we know that $X_{t+h}-X_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\nu h, \zeta^2 h\right)$. We calculate moments of the random variable $X_{t+h}-X_t$ using the moment generating function of the normal distribution, and find for the fourth moment that
    \[
        \mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{t+h}-X_t\right|^4\right] & =M^{\prime \prime \prime \prime}(0) \\
        & =\nu^4 h^4+\nu^3 \xi^2 h^3+3 \xi^4 h^2 \\
        & =\left(\nu^4 h^2+\nu^3 \xi^2 h+3 \xi^4\right) h^2 \\
        & \leq\left(\nu^4 T^2+\nu^3 \xi^2 T+3 \xi^4\right) h^2
        .
    \]
    Therefore, for $\alpha=4$, $\beta=1$ and $\gamma=\nu^4 T^2+\nu^3 \xi^2 T+3 \xi^4$, we satisfy \Cref{thm:kolmogorov_continuity_criterion}, and so there exists a modification of the stochastic process $\Xt$ that has almost-surely continuous paths.
\end{proof}

Using \Cref{prop:continuous_modification_of_noise}, we can now extend our discrete-time approximation of the noise term in \Cref{def:discrete_time_noise_process} to the continuous-time limit. This modification is known as the Wiener process, or Brownian motion, and is denoted by $W_t$.

\begin{definition}[Brownian Motion]
    A real-valued stochastic process $\left\{W_t\right\}_{t \geq 0}$ is a (standard) Brownian motion starting at $x \in \mathbb{R}$ if:
    \begin{align}
    (P0) \quad & W_0=x \\
    (P1) \quad & W_t-W_s \sim N(0, t-s) \\
    (P2) \quad & \text{if } 0=t_0 \leq t_1 \leq t_2 \leq \cdots \leq t_n \text{ then } \\
    & W_{t_1}-W_{t_0}, W_{t_2}-W_{t_1}, \ldots, W_{t_n}-W_{t_{n-1}} \text{ are independent,} \\
    (P3) \quad & t \mapsto W_t \text{ is continuous, almost surely.}
    \end{align}
\end{definition}

Further, as we were doing with stochastic processes before, we can consider a filtered probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in [0, \infty)}, \mathbb{P})$ where $W_t$ is adapted to the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in [0, \infty)}$. We have the following nice property of Brownian Motion:

\begin{lemma}{Adapted Wiener Process is a Martingale}
    The $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted Wiener process $W_t$ is a martingale with respect to the filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \in [0, \infty)}$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
    In order to prove this, we must show that $\mathbb{E}\left(W_t \mid \mathcal{F}_s\right)=W_s$ for any $t>s$. But as $W_s$ is $\mathcal{F}_s$-measurable (by adaptedness) this is equivalent to $\mathbb{E}\left(W_t-W_s \mid \mathcal{F}_s\right)=0$, and this is clearly true by the definition of the Wiener process (as $W_t-W_s$ has zero mean and is independent of $\mathcal{F}_s$ ).
\end{proof}

Intuitively, we can see that Brownian Motion is the continuous limit of variables that are represented as sums of i.i.d. normal random variables. Given our original assumptions about the i.i.d.ness of $Z_k$, we can see that in the continuous limit of $\delta t \rightarrow 0$, we have
\[
\sum_{s_i \leq t} Z_{s_i} \rightarrow \int_{s \leq t} Z_s \mathrm{~d} s \approx W_t
. 
\]
Therefore, the noise would ideally be defined as some notion of the "derivative" of Brownian Motion $\mathrm{d} W_t$, giving us the SDE from \Cref{eq:sde_intuitive_2} as
\[
\frac{d X_t}{d t}=\mu\left(X_t\right)+\sigma\left(X_t\right) \times \mathrm{d} W_t
. \label{eq:sde_with_dW}
\]
Unfortunately, we hit a roadblock when we try to define the noise as $\mathrm{d} W_t$. The Wiener process is not differentiable in the traditional sense, and so we need to define a new notion of the derivative of the Wiener process. This is where Itô calculus comes in.

\section{Itô Calculus}

Let us rewrite \Cref{eq:sde_with_dW} as an integral equation, giving us
\[
    X_t=X_0+\int_0^t \mu\left(X_s\right) \mathrm{d} s+\int_0^t \sigma\left(X_s\right) \mathrm{d} W_s \label{eq:integral_form_of_sde}
\]
The first term on the right-hand side is a Riemann-Stieltjes integral, and can be defined using standard rules of integration. The second term, however, is undefined in the traditional sense, since the integrand itself is a "noisy process" and has unbound total variation. As a simple example, consider discretising the integral of Brownian Motion itself over the interval $[0, t]$, giving us
\[
    \int_0^t W_s \mathrm{~d} W_s \approx \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} W_{t_k^{\star}}\left[W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\right], \quad t_k^{\star} \in\left[t_k, t_{k+1}\right]
    .
\]
For a Riemann-Stieltjes integral, we can pick any point $t_k^{\star}$ in the interval $[t_k, t_{k+1}]$ to approximate the integral, as long as the upper and lower Darboux sums converge to the same value. However, with some simple algebra, we can see that this is not the case. Considering $t_k^{\star} = t_k$, we have
\[
    \int_0^t W_s \mathrm{~d} W_s &\approx \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} W_{t_k}\left[W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\right] \\
    &=\frac{1}{2} W_t^2-\frac{1}{2} W_0^2-\frac{1}{2}\langle W\rangle_t \\
    &=\frac{1}{2} W_t^2-\frac{1}{2} W_0^2-\frac{t}{2}
    .
\]
Instead, if we ``peak'' into the future and consider $t_k^{\star} = t_{k+1}$, we have
\[
    \int_0^t W_s \mathrm{~d} W_s &\approx \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} W_{t_{k+1}}\left[W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}\right] \\
    &=\frac{1}{2} W_t^2-\frac{1}{2} W_0^2+\frac{t}{2}
    .
\]
This mismatch raises a big issue with how we might be able to define stochastic integrals, as it is no longer the case that the partitioned sums should converge to a single value regardless of the choice of $t_k^{\star}$. In fact, different choices to $t_k^{\star}$ result in the corresponding processes satisfying different fundamental properties as well. the first choice of $t_k^{\star}=t_k$ results in the integral being an \textit{Itô integral}, and the second choice of $t_k^{\star}=t_{k+1}$ results in a \textit{Stratanovich integral}.

\subsection{Construction of Itô Integrals}

We have defined the Itô integral for Brownian motion, having decided which point in an interval to pick (i.e. the leftmost). In order to extend the definition of the Itô integral to more general stochastic processes, we can follow the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Defining the Itô integral for simple step-wise processes
    \item Proving the Itô isometry for simple processes
    \item Constructing a sequence of simple processes that converge to a general stochastic process in a mean-square sense
    \item Defining the Itô integral as the limit of the sequence of simple processes
\end{enumerate}

For the sake of brevity, we refer to \textcite[Pg. 21, Section 3.1]{oksendal2013stochastic} for this construction, choosing here to state the important properties and definitions, namely the Itô isometry and the Itô integral. First, we define the family of processes that we will consider, namely processes that are bounded and adapted.

\begin{definition}[Class of Bounded and Adapted Processes]
    \label{def:class_of_bounded_and_adapted_processes}
    Let $\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{V}(S, T)$ be the class of functions
    \[
        f(t, \omega):[0, \infty) \times \Omega \rightarrow \mathbf{R}
    \]
    such that
    (i) $(t, \omega) \rightarrow f(t, \omega)$ is $\mathcal{B} \times \mathcal{F}$-measurable, where $\mathcal{B}$ denotes the Borel $\sigma$-algebra on $[0, \infty)$.
    (ii) $f(t, \omega)$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-adapted.
    (iii) $E\left[\int_S^T f(t, \omega)^2 d t\right]<\infty$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Itô Integral]
    Let $V_t$ be a bounded and adapted process as per \Cref{def:class_of_bounded_and_adapted_processes}. Then there exists a sequence of simple processes $\left(V_t^n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ such that
    \[
        \lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T\left(V_u-V_u^n\right)^2 \mathrm{~d} u\right]=0
    \]
    The Itô integral then exists (in $\mathcal{L}^2(\mathbb{P})$) and is defined as
    \[
        I:=\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \int_0^T V_u^n \mathrm{~d} W_u,
    \]
    which is independent of the choice of the sequence $\left(V_t^n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$.
\end{definition}
Finally, we state the Itô isometry, which is a key property of the Itô integral.

\begin{definition}[Itô Isometry]
    \[
        E\left[\left(\int_S^T f(t, \omega) d B_t\right)^2\right]=E\left[\int_S^T f^2(t, \omega) d t\right] \quad \text { for all } f \in \mathcal{V}(S, T).
    \]
\end{definition}

\subsection{Itô Processes and Itô's Lemma}

Now that we've been able to define the Itô integral $\int V_t \mathrm{~d} W_t$ for more general stochastic processes, we can consider other properties of calculus that are essential for any meaningful analysis, such as the chain rule.
Given a stochastic process defined as
\[
    X_t=X_0+\int_0^t a_s\left(X_s\right) \mathrm{d} s+\int_0^t V_s \mathrm{~d} W_s
\]
or as a stochastic differential equation,
\[
    \mathrm{d} X_t=a_t\left(X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t+V_t \mathrm{~d} W_t \label{eq:ito_process}
\]
$X_t$ here is itself a stochastic process defined as a combination of an Itô integral and an ordinary integral, and we term it an \textit{Itô process}\footnote{The term "Itô process" is used to describe a stochastic process that can be expressed as an Itô integral. Frequently, the term is used interchangeably with "stochastic integral" or "stochastic process".}.

Can we calculate what the stochastic process $f\left(X_t\right)$ would look like? Yes, in fact it can be shown that there exists a version of chain rule for stochastic processes, and it is known as Itô's lemma. First, we consider a simpleversion, where we have $X_t=W_t$ and $a_t=0$. Then we have

\begin{definition}[Stochastic Chain Rule]
    Suppose $f \in C^2([0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R})$ (i.e. $f(t, x)$ is twice continuously differentiable in $\left.x\right)$. Then $Y_t:=f\left(W_t\right)$ is an Itô process, and
    \[
        \mathrm{d} Y_t=\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\left(W_t\right) \mathrm{d} t+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(W_t\right) \mathrm{d} W_t
    \]
\end{definition}

We can extend this to the general case where $X_t$ is an Itô process given by \Cref{eq:ito_process}, and $f \in C^2([0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R})$. In fact, the proof will follow quite similarly as above, where we need to make sense of convergence in mean and in probability, and apply the same principles. The more popular (and more general) version of Itô's lemma is given below.

\begin{theorem}[Itô's Lemma]
    Let $X_t$ be an Itô process given by \Cref{eq:ito_process}, and $f \in C^2([0, \infty) \times \mathbb{R})$. Then $Y_t:=f\left(X_t\right)$ is an Itô process, and is defined as
    \[
    \mathrm{d} Y_t=\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\left(t, X_t\right)+a_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(t, X_t\right)+\frac{1}{2} V_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\left(t, X_t\right)\right) \mathrm{d} t+V_t \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(t, X_t\right) \mathrm{d} W_t .
    \]
    Alternatively, we can write this as
    \[
        \mathrm{d} Y_t=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\left(t, X_t\right) \mathrm{d} t+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\left(t, X_t\right) \mathrm{d} X_t+\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\left(t, X_t\right)\left(\mathrm{d} X_t\right)^2
    \]
\end{theorem}

\section{Path Measures and their Laws of Probability}

We can now write down the solution to the SDE in \Cref{eq:integral_form_of_sde} as
\[
    \d X_t = \mu\left(X_t\right) \d t + \sigma\left(X_t\right) \d W_t,
\]
where it can be readily seen that the solution is an Itô process. If we were to consider the probability distribution over a discrete subset of random variables indexed by $t_0, t_1, \ldots, t_n$ along the ``path'' of this process, we could write the joint distribution as
\[
    \mathbb{P}\left(X_{t_0} \in A_0, X_{t_1} \in A_1, \ldots, X_{t_n} \in A_n\right),
\]
where $A_i \subset \mathbb{R}$ is a Borel set. This is known as the law of the process. In the continuous limit, where we have a (potentially) infinite number of random variables indexed by functions belonging to $\mathcal{T} = C([0, T], \R^n)$, we define the corresponding probability measures as \textit{path measures}.

\begin{definition}[Path Measures]
    Let $\mathcal{T} = C([0, T], \R^n)$ be the space of continuous functions from $[0, T]$ to $\R^n$, and $\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T})$ be the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $\mathcal{T}$. Then a path measure is a probability measure on the space $(\mathcal{T}, \mathcal{B}(\mathcal{T}))$.
\end{definition}

Therefore, for solutions of SDEs of the form in \Cref{eq:integral_form_of_sde}, we can define the \textit{law of the process} as a path measure, notated as $\mathbb{P} = \operatorname{Law}(\Xt)$.

\begin{definition}[Path Measure of an Itô Process]
    Let $\Xt$ be an Itô process defined as the solution to the SDE in \Cref{eq:integral_form_of_sde}, with drift coefficient $\mu$ and diffusion coefficient $\sigma$. Further, let $\pi_0$ be the marginal distribution of the RV at $t=0$. Then the path measure of $\Xt$ is denoted as $\mathbb{P}^{\mu, \pi_0} = \operatorname{Law}(\Xt)$.
\end{definition}

In this section, we discuss certain properties of path measures that could be considered ``laws of probability'' for operations on paths of an Itô process. These properties are often continuous-time analogues of the standard laws of probability, and will be very useful in the upcoming chapters. 

\subsection{Disintegration Theorem: ``Pathwise'' Product Rule}

In traditional probability, the product rule for the joint distribution of random variables $X$ and $Y$ is written quite simply as $p(x, y) = p(x \mid y) p(y)$. For path measures defined on the space of paths of an Itô process, it is possible to write a similar rule, known as the \textit{disintegration theorem}. First, let us define some notation.


\begin{definition}[Product Spaces and Canonical Projection Operator]
    \label{def:product_spaces_and_canonical_projection_operator}
    Let $(\Omega_x, \mathcal{F}_x)$ and $(\Omega_y, \mathcal{F}_y)$ be two measurable spaces. In the product space $\Omega_x \times \Omega_y$, we define a measurable rectangle the set of the form $A_x \times A_y$, where $A_x \in \mathcal{F}_x$ and $A_y \in \mathcal{F}_y$. Then, the $\sigma$-algebra of $\Omega_x \times \Omega_y$ is defined as the $\sigma$-algebra generated by all measurable rectangles, resulting in the product space $(\Omega_x \times \Omega_y, \mathcal{F}_x \otimes \mathcal{F}_y)$. The canonical projection operators are measurable functions defined as
    \[
        \pi_x:& \ \Omega \to \Omega_x, \quad \pi_x(\omega_x, \omega_y) = \omega_x, \quad \forall \  \omega_x \in \Omega_x, \omega_y \in \Omega_y \\
        \pi_y:& \ \Omega \to \Omega_y, \quad \pi_y(\omega_x, \omega_y) = \omega_y, \quad \forall \  \omega_x \in \Omega_x, \omega_y \in \Omega_y
        .
    \]
\end{definition}

We can now state the disintegration theorem as follows:

\begin{theorem}[Disintegration Theorem]
    Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{B}(\Omega), \P)$ be a product space where $\Omega = \Omega_x \times \Omega_y$, and a canonical projection operator $\pi_x$ and $\pi_y$ be defined as in \Cref{def:product_spaces_and_canonical_projection_operator}. Then, there exists a probability measure $\P_{y \mid x}(\cdot \mid x)$ on $(\Omega_x, \mathcal{F}_x)$ such that
    \[
        \int_{\Omega_x \times \Omega_y} f(x, y) \d \P(x, y) = \int_{\Omega_x} \int_{\Omega_y} f(x, y) \d \P_{y \mid x} (y \mid x) \d \P_x (\pi_x^{-1}(x)),
    \]
    where we denote $\P_x = \P(\pi_x^{-1}(x))$ as the marginal probability measure and $\P_{y \mid x}$ as the conditional probability measure. 
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Girsanov's Theorem: ``Pathwise'' Change of Measure}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{"/Users/shreyaspadhy/Documents/Obsidian Vault/phd-thesis/Chapter3/Figs/Vector/sde_girsanov.pdf"}
    \caption{An example of a change of measure using Girsanov's theorem. On the left, we plot the solution to an SDE $\d X_t = a_t X_t \d t + \sigma_t \d W_t$, with $a_t = 1$ and $\sigma_t = 0.5$ along with the Brownian motion $\d W_t$ defined on the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$. On the right, we plot the same SDE under a change of measure, where we have a new Brownian motion $\d \hat{W}_t$ defined on the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{Q})$. Under this new measure, the process $\d \hat{W}_t = \d W_t + \frac{a_t}{\sigma_t} \d t$ is a standard Brownian motion, and the process $X_t$ can be written as $\d X_t = \sigma_t \d \hat{W}_t$. The original Brownian motion now has a negative drift.}
    \label{fig:sde_girsanov}
\end{figure}

We have discussed the Radon-Nikodym derivative previously, as the ratio of two probability measures, and a function that allows us to express one probability measure in terms of another (usually the Lebesgue measure). Unfortunately, for path measures that are laws of SDEs, there doesn't exist a simple expression for the Radon-Nikodym derivative that maps between the path measure to the Lebesgue measure. However, we are still able to represent the density of two path measures corresponding to two Itô processes, which allows us to perform a ``pathwise'' change of measure. As we will soon see, the ability to write down the density ratio between two path measures allows us to calculate expectations of functions of paths of an Itô process with respect to the law of another, much simpler, Itô process, which is a very useful property for importance sampling, and for representing solutions to SDEs in terms of simpler processes.

Here, we introduce Girsanov's theorem, a groundbreaking result in the theory of stochastic processes, which allows us to represent the density of two path measures corresponding to two Itô processes, which allows us to perform a ``pathwise'' change of measure. Girsanov's theorem is originally stated in a somewhat different form, which we discuss for completeness, before stating useful corollaries that are more suited for our discussion. Intuitively, Girsanov's Theorem states that if we change the drift coefficient of an Itô process, the law of the process does not change dramatically, and the law of the new process is absolutely continuous with respect to the law of the original process, and can be computed explicitly in terms of their Radon-Nikodym derivative. 

We first state the original theorem, which is a bit more general than what we will need, and then state a corollary that is more suited for our discussion.

\begin{theorem}[Girsanov's Theorem]
    \label{thm:girsanovs}
    Let $X_t$ be an Itô process defined on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ as the solution to the SDE 
    \[
        \d X_t = a(t, X_t) \d t + \sigma(t, X_t) \d W_t, \quad X_0 = x.
    \]
    Suppose there exist $u(t, x)$ and $v(t, x)$ such that
    \[
        u(t, x) \sigma(t, x) = a(t, x) - v(t, x), \quad \forall \ t \in [0, T], \ x \in \mathbb{R}^n.
    \]
    and $u(t, x)$ satisfies Novikov's condition, given by
    \[
        \E\left[\exp \left(\frac{1}{2} \int_0^T u(s, X_s)^2 \d s\right)\right]<\infty. \label{eq:novikov_condition}
    \]
    Then, under the probability measure $\mathbb{Q}$ defined by the Radon-Nikodym derivative
    \[
        \frac{\d \mathbb{Q}}{\d \mathbb{P}} = \exp \left(\int_0^T u(s, X_s) \d W_s - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T u(s, X_s)^2 \d s \right),
    \]
    the process 
    \[
        \hat{W}_t = W_t + \int_0^t u(s, X_s) \d s
    \]
    is a Wiener process under $\mathbb{Q}$, and in terms of the process $\hat{W}_t$, the process $X_t$ can be written as
    \[
        \d X_t = v(t, X_t) \d t + \sigma(t, X_t) \d \hat{W}_t.
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    See \textcite[Theorem 8.6.6, Pg. 165]{oksendal2013stochastic}.
\end{proof}

As a concrete example of applying \Cref{thm:girsanovs}, we can see how an Itô process can be written as a Brownian motion under a change of measure.

\begin{example}[Itô Process as Brownian Motion]
    Let $X_t$ be an Itô process defined on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ as the solution to the SDE 
    \[
        \d X_t = a_t(X_t) \d t + \sigma_t \d W_t, \quad X_0 = x.
    \]
    Then, from \Cref{thm:girsanovs}, with a choice of $u(t, x) = \frac{a_t}{\sigma_t}$ and $v(t, x) = 0$, we have that
    $\hat{W}_t$ is a Brownian motion defined as 
    \[
        \d \hat{W}_t = \d W_t - \frac{a_t}{\sigma_t} \d t, \quad \hat{W}_0 = 0,
    \]
    under the probability measure $\mathbb{Q}$ which is defined through the Radon-Nikodym derivative
    \[
        \frac{\d \mathbb{Q}}{\d \mathbb{P}} = \exp \left(\int_0^T \frac{a_s}{\sigma_s} \d W_s - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \left(\frac{a_s}{\sigma_s}\right)^2 \d s \right), \quad 0 \leq t \leq T,
    \]
    and $X_t$ can be written as a scaled Brownian motion under $\mathbb{Q}$ as
    \[
        \d X_t = \sigma_t \d \hat{W}_t, \quad X_0 = x.
    \]
\end{example}

We can further state a more general version of Girsanov's theorem between general Itô processes that are solutions to SDEs as follows:

\begin{theorem}[Girsanov's Theorem for RND Between SDEs]
    Given Itô processes $X_t$ and $Y_t$ defined as the solutions to the SDEs
    \[
        \d X_t = a_t(X_t) \d t + \sigma_t \d W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \mu,
    \]
    and
    \[
        \d Y_t = b_t(Y_t) \d t + \nu_t \d W_t, \quad Y_0 \sim \nu,
    \]
    with path measures $\P^{a, \mu}$ and $\P^{b, \nu}$ respectively, then we have
    \[
        \ln \left(\frac{\d\P^{a, \mu}}{\d \P^{b, \nu}}\right)(Z) = \ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \mu}{\mathrm{d} \nu}\right)\left(Z_0\right) + \int_0^T \sigma_t^{-2}\left(a_t-b_t\right)\left(Z_t\right) \cdot \mathrm{d} Z_t + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \sigma_t^{-2}\left(b_t^2-a_t^2\right)\left(Z_t\right) \mathrm{d} t
        .
    \]
\end{theorem}

With the ability to write down the density ratio between two path measures, we are now able to define well-known concepts from standard probability theory, but in the context of path measures. For example, we can define the KL divergence between two path measures as follows:

\begin{definition}[KL Divergence Between Path Measures]
    Let $\P^{a, \mu}$ and $\P^{b, \nu}$ be two path measures for Itô processes $X_t$ and $Y_t$ defined as the solutions to the SDEs with drift coefficients $a_t$ and $b_t$ respectively and diffusion coefficient $\sigma_t$. Then the KL divergence between $\P^{a, \mu}$ and $\P^{b, \nu}$ is defined as
    \[
     \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\P^{a, \mu} \| \P^{b, \nu}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}^{a, \mu}} \left[\ln \left(\frac{\mathrm{d} \mu}{\mathrm{d} \nu}\right) (X_0) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \sigma_t^{-2} \|a_t-b_t\|^2({X}_t) \mathrm{d} t\right]
     .
    \]
\end{definition}

Another useful property of Itô processes is the ability to condition an Itô process on a set of paths. This is particularly useful for solving SDEs with boundary conditions, which are SDEs that have constraints on the values of the process at the boundaries of the domain. Here, we discuss Doob's $h$-transform, a tool that allows us to define Itô processes conditioned on boundary conditions as the solution to an SDE with an additional term in the drift coefficient.

\begin{definition}[Doob's $h$-transform]
    Let $X_t$ be an Itô process defined as the solution to the SDE
    \[
        \d X_t = a_t(X_t) \d t + \sigma_t \d W_t, \quad X_0 = x.
    \]
    It then follows that the conditioned process $X_t$ conditioned on a boundary condition $X_T \in B$ is defined as the solution to the SDE
    \[
        \d X_t = \left(a_t(X_t) + \sigma_t^2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln \P \left(X_T \in B \mid X_t\right)\right) \d t + \sigma_t \d W_t, \quad X_0 = x,
    \]
    where the term $h(X_t, t) \triangleq \P \left(X_T \in B \mid X_t\right)$ is defined as the $h$-transform, and we have $\P(X_T \in B) = 1$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proof}
    See \textcite[Section 7.5, Pg. 113]{sarkka2019applied}.
\end{proof}

We have discussed some very useful properties of path measures, and techniques to perform ``pathwise'' operations on Itô processes, such as conditioning and a change of measure. We now turn our attention to one of the seminal results in stochastic calculus, which has enabled their application in generative modelling and diffusion models, known as the time-reversal of SDEs.

As we alluded to in the previous chapter, generative modelling algorithms such as score-matching and DDPMs simultaneously design the ``forward'' process $q_{\theta}(z \mid x)$ that starts from an unknown data distribution $\hat{\pi}$ and iteratively noises it until a tractable noise distribution $\pi_T$, and the ``backward'' process $p_{\phi}(x \mid z)$ that starts from the noise distribution $\pi_T$ and iteratively removes noise from it until the data distribution $\hat{\pi}$. An alluring property of SDEs is that we can choose a specific form of the ``forward'' process $q_{\theta}$ as a simple SDE, and due to the time-reversal property of SDEs, we are able to write down the ``backward'' process $p{\phi}$ in closed form. Anderson and Nelson showed that it is possible to write down a ``backward'' Itô process that is the time-reversal of the forward process, so that the marginal distributions of the forward and backward processes are the same at the same time. 

There are two distinct approaches on how to define the time-reversal SDE, both of which have subtle differences that can impact the theoretical properties of SDEs that we can work with. The first approach, which we title as ``forward-time'', defines the time-reversal SDE on the same filtration as the original forward process, with the same ``forward-time'' Brownian motion as the stochastic term. This approach allows us to use our discussed tools such as Girsanov's theorem, and continue to use the definition of the Itô integral with respect to the forward-time Brownian motion. 

The second approach, which we title as ``reverse-time''; defines the time-reversal in a manner that might be more intuitive to consider from a theoretical perspective, but is more difficult to work with from a practical perspective due to the lack of current tooling. In this approach, we first define a ``reverse-time'' Brownian motion, which is a Brownian motion that integrates backwards in time, starting from the end-point of the original forward process. We then define the time-reversal SDE on a different filtration, which is the filtration of the reverse-time Brownian motion. 

\begin{definition}[Time-reversal of SDEs with forward time]
    \label{def:anderson-reversal}
    Given an Itô process $X_t$ adapted to the ``forward-time'' filtration $\mathcal{F}_t=\sigma\left\{X_s, W_s: 0 \leq s \leq t\right\}$ which is defined as the solution to the SDE
    \[
        \d X_t = \mu_t(X_t) \d t + \sigma_t(X_t) \d W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \hat{\pi},
    \]
    we introduce a re-indexed stochastic process $Z_t := X_{T-t}, t \in [0, T]$ adapted to the same ``forward-time'' filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$. Then, $Z_t$ is the solution to the following SDE
    \[
        \d Z_t = \left[ -\mu_{T-t}(Z_t) + \sigma_{T-t}^{\top} \sigma_{T-t} \nabla_{Z_t} \ln P_{T-t}(Z_t) \right] \d t + \sigma_{T-t}(Z_t) \d W_t, \quad Z_0 \sim \pi_T
    \]
\end{definition}

There are some important properties of this time-reversal to note:
\begin{itemize}
    \item By definition, $Z_t$ is adapted to the same ``forward-time'' filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$ as the original process $X_t$. This means that we solve $Z_t$ in the forward-time sense, with a Brownian motion $W_t$ that is adapted to the same filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$.
    \item However, due to the re-indexing of the random variable $Z_t$, the joint law of the stochastic process \textit{is not the same} as the joint law of the original process $X_t$. This is an important property to note, as the ``forward-time'' time-reversal matches the marginal distributions of $X_t$ and $Z_t$, but not the joint law.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Time-reversal of SDEs with reverse time]
    \label{def:nelson-reversal}
    Given an Itô process $X_t$ adapted to the ``forward-time'' filtration $\mathcal{F}_t=\sigma\left\{X_s, W_s: 0 \leq s \leq t\right\}$ which is defined as the solution to the SDE
    \[
        \d X_t = \mu_t(X_t) \d t + \sigma_t(X_t) \d W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \hat{\pi},
    \]
    we then define the \textit{same} random variable $Y_t := X_t, t \in [0, T]$ defined backwards in time, and adapted to the ``reverse-time'' filtration $\mathcal{G}_t=\sigma\left\{X_s, W_s: T \geq s \geq t\right\}$. Then, $Y_t$ is the solution to the following SDE solved backwards in time from $t=T$ to $t=0$
    \[
        \d Y_t = \left[ \mu_{t}(Y_t) - \sigma_{t}^{\top} \sigma_{t} \nabla_{Y_t} \ln P_{t}(Y_t) \right] \d t + \sigma_{t}(Y_t) \d \overleftarrow{W}_t, \quad Y_T \sim \pi_T,
    \]
    where $\overleftarrow{W}_t$ is a Brownian motion that is adapted to the ``reverse-time'' filtration $\mathcal{G}_t$.
\end{definition}

Some important properties of the ``reverse-time'' time-reversal to note:
\begin{itemize}
    \item By definition, $Y_t$ is adapted to the ``reverse-time'' filtration $\mathcal{G}_t$, and not the ``forward-time'' filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$. This means that we solve $Y_t$ in the reverse-time sense, with a Brownian motion $\overleftarrow{W}_t$ that is adapted to the ``reverse-time'' filtration $\mathcal{G}_t$.
    \item Here, however, the joint law of the stochastic process is the same as the joint law of the original process $X_t$. This is an important property to note, as the ``reverse-time'' time-reversal matches the joint law of $X_t$ and $Y_t$, i.e. $\operatorname{Law}\left(\left\{X_t\right\}_{0 \leq t \leq T}\right)=\operatorname{Law}\left(\left\{Y_t\right\}_{0 \leq t \leq T}\right)$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Theoretical Properties of Time-Reversals}
    Depending on the choice of time-reversal, there are certain mathematical properties from stochastic calculus that are preserved, and certain properties that are not preserved. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Girsanov's theorem: While we didn't discuss the proof of Girsanov's theorem in this chapter, an important property that is used in the proof is that the processes need to be adapted to the same filtration. Therefore, the ``forward-time'' time-reversal allows us to use Girsanov's theorem between the forward and backward processes.
        \item Mixing an Itô integral that is forward-adapted (to $\mathcal{F}_t$) with an Itô integral that is reverse-adapted (to $\mathcal{G}_t$) breaks Burkholder–Davis–Gundy inequalities and Itô isometry. Hence expectations and 
        $L^2$ bounds that are routine in the forward‑only world become really difficult to work with in the ``reverse-time'' world.
        \item However, in our framework for generative modelling and sampling, we are really interested in learning our parametrised maps $p_{\phi}$ and $q_{\theta}$ under the framework of probability divergences that we want to write between two path measures $\P$ and $\Q$, corresponding to the forward and backward processes respectively. In this situtation, the ``reverse-time'' time-reversal allows us to write the KL divergence between the forward and backward processes easily, as we will readily minimise the KL divergence between the forward and ``reverse-time'' backward processes when we use the ``reverse-time'' time-reversal, since their joint law is the same.
    \end{itemize}
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Reverse-Time SDEs and Föllmer's Itô Calculus}

We have seen that the ``reverse-time'' SDE framework provides the same joint law for the forward and reverse SDEs, which would enable us to define probability divergences between the forward and reverse SDEs. However, since the forward and reverse SDEs exist on different filtrations going in different directions, we cannot rely on our existing machinery of Ito integrals and Girsanov's theorem, bringing us to a standstill. 

In this section, we introduce an alternative framework, Föllmer's Itô calculus, that allows us to derive a version of Itô's formula for SDEs without relying on the existence of filtrations. While there are some subtleties to this framework, and the results might be not as general as the existing framework, for our purposes, it suffices to use this framework to define a novel Radon-Nikodym derivative between the forward and reverse processes, which we will use to define a novel Girsanov's theorem in the next chapter.

\begin{definition}[Ito's formula without probability]
    \label{def:ito-formula-without-probability}
    (\textcite[Theorem 6.6, Pg. 122]{eberle2011introduction}, \textcite{follmer2006calcul}) Suppose that $X: [0, T) \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is a continuous function with continuous quadratic variation $\langle X \rangle_t$ w.r.t a partition $(\pi_n)$. Then, for any $f \in C^2([0, t) \times \mathbb{R}^d)$, and for any $t \in [0, T)$, the Itô integral
    \[
        \int_0^t \nabla f(s, X_s) \d X_s := \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{s \in \pi_n, s < t} \nabla f(s, X_s) (X_{s^{\prime} \wedge t} - X_s)
    \]
    exists, and Itô's formula
    \[
        f(X_t) = f(X_0) + \int_0^t \nabla f(s, X_s) \d X_s + \frac{1}{2} \nabla^2 f(s, X_s) \d \langle X \rangle_s
    \]
    holds. 
\end{definition}

We note here that \Cref{def:ito-formula-without-probability} holds only for individual continuous functions (or paths) $X_t$ with continuous quadratic variation, and not for general processes adapted to a filtration. Therefore, we no longer require the existence of a filtration to define Itô's formula. However, it is important to note that the result is dependent on the specific partition of the interval $[0, t)$ that we choose, and if the quadratic variation of the function $X_t$ varies with the partition, then the result may not hold. This is not a problem for our purposes, aas the quadratic variation of the Wiener process is deterministic, finite and continuous regardless of the partition almost surely (\textcite[Pg. 120]{eberle2011introduction}).

In Föllmer's framework, defining the ``reverse-time'' Itô integral is done by using the same definition of the Itô integral as in the forward-time case.

\begin{definition}[``Reverse-time'' Itô's formula without probability]
    \label{def:ito-formula-without-probability-reverse}
    (\textcite[Theorem 6.7, Pg. 123]{eberle2011introduction}, \textcite{follmer2006calcul,kunitha1982backward}) Suppose that $X: [0, T) \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is a continuous function with continuous quadratic variation $\langle X \rangle_t$ w.r.t a partition $(\pi_n)$. Then, for any $f \in C^2([0, t) \times \mathbb{R}^d)$, and for any $t \in [0, T)$, the backward Itô integral
    \[
        \int_0^t \nabla f(s, X_s) \d \overleftarrow{X}_s := \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{s \in \pi_n, s < t} \nabla f(s^{\prime} \wedge t, X_s) (X_{s^{\prime} \wedge t} - X_s)
    \]
    exists, and Itô's formula
    \[
        f(X_t) = f(X_0) + \int_0^t \nabla f(s, X_s) \d \overleftarrow{X}_s - \frac{1}{2} \nabla^2 f(s, X_s) \d \langle X \rangle_s
    \]
    holds. 
\end{definition}
