The task of sampling in machine learning can be broadly divided into three distinct paradigms. In the first situation, we don't have any existing samples from an unnormalised probability distribution, and we are only able to evaluate the unnormalised density $\hpi(x)$ and the gradient of the density $\nabla_x \hpi(x)$\footnote{The assumption is often made that one can evaluate the gradient of the log-density $\nabla_x \log \hpi$. These are equivalent assumptions, as by the chain rule of calculus we can write $\nabla_x \log \hpi(x) = \frac{1}{\hpi(x)} \nabla_x \hpi(x)$}. The task of \textit{unnormalised density sampling} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, I.) focuses on learning to draw samples from this probability distribution (and often-times using these samples to estimate the normalising constant). 

In the second task of \textit{generative modelling} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, II.), we only have access to a training dataset of samples from our probability distribution of interest, i.e. $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}$, but not the ability to evaluate the density or its gradients. The task therefore focuses on using these samples to learn parametrised approximations to our probability distribution of interest, allowing us to draw novel samples from this approximation, and sometimes allowing us to estimate the density $\hpi(x)$. 

Finally, in the third task of \textit{conditional sampling}, we consider two distinct subcategories. In the first situation, we already have access to a parametrised approximation to the distribution of interest $\hpi$, and we wish to learn a parametrised approximation to a conditional distribution $\pi_{. \mid y}$ that is specified through the definition of a likelihood term $p(y \mid x)$, for some paired observations $y$. We call this the task of \textit{conditional generative modeling} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, III.A). In the second situation, we have access to samples from the unconditional distribution $\hpi$, and we wish to learn how to modify these samples to instead belong to the conditional distribution $\pi_{. \mid y}$. We call this the task of \textit{``pathwise'' conditioning} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, III.B), where the term ``path'' refers to the fact that we are modifying individual samples, rather than the parametrised approximation itself. Usually, the ``pathwise'' conditioning setup is only possible when the unconditional distribution $\hpi$ as well as the likelihood term $p(y \mid x)$ belong to a simple family of distributions, such as Gaussian distributions. In the task of Bayesian inference, practitioners may recognise conditional sampling as the task of posterior inference, where the posterior distribution $\pi_{. \mid y} \propto p(y \mid x) \hpi(x)$.

\section{A Unifying Variational Framework}

In this thesis, we will focus on a framework for designing sampling algorithms that has found great success across a variety of algorithms, whether implicitly or as an explicit design. In simple terms, we design (or learn) a mapping between a simple base distribution $\mu$ and the distribution of interest $\hpi$. By design, it is easy to obtain samples $z \sim \mu$, and we attempt to learn a mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ that transports these samples to samples from the distribution of interest $\hpi$. In many algorithms, it is actually simpler to first design a ``noising'', i.e. a reverse mapping $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ that takes samples $x \sim \hpi$ and maps them to the simpler distribution $\mu$. Under this framework, the choice of $\mu$ and the mappings $p_{\theta}$ and $q_{\phi}$ cover a large family of algorithms, allowing for an infinite number of valid mappings to exist. In order to constrain our solution space to tractable algorithms, we usually will make a choice for $\mu$ that is easy to sample from, and the mappings $p_{\theta}$ and $q_{\phi}$ are chosen to be parametrised functions that are easy to manipulate and compute. In this setup, it is then natural to consider a loss function of the following form to learn the mappings
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:divergence_loss}
    \mathcal{L}_D(\theta, \phi) = \mathcal{D}\left( q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mu(z) \mid \mid p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \hpi(x) \right).
\end{equation}
For this thesis, we make a specific choice of a heirarchical latent variable model for our mappings $p_{\theta}$ and $q_{\phi}$. While this choice might seem arbitrary, as we see later, a large family of existing algorithms fall under this framework, including Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) algorithms \parencite{neal2001annealed,jarzynski1997equilibrium,jarzynski1997nonequilibrium}, Score Matching algorithms \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score}, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) \parencite{ho2020denoising}. 

We make the choice of $x_0 := z$ and $x_T := x$ with a series of intermediate latent variables $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}\}$. The mappings $p_{\theta}$ and $q_{\phi}$ are then chosen to be simple and easy to compute in a heirarchical manner as
\[
    p_{\theta}(x_T, \ldots, x_1 \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} p_{\theta_{t}}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t}), \quad q_{\phi}(x_0, \ldots, x_{T-1} \mid x_T) = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} q_{\phi_{t}}(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}).
\]
While we can choose arbitrarily complex functions to define the intermediate mappings $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$, as $T \to \infty$ and we consider the continuous limit, even a simple choice for $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$ such as Gaussian distributions can yield highly flexible mappings. Furthermore, making a Gaussian choice for $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$ allows us to write down the continuous-time limit of the mappings as SDEs, which will enable us to use the rich mathematical framework of stochastic calculus to develop more flexible algorithms later. The Gaussian choice for $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$ is given by
\[
    p_{\theta_t}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t+1}; x_{t} + f_{\theta_t}(x_t), \sigma_t^2 I), \quad q_{\phi_t}(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t}; x_{t+1} + b_{\phi_t}(x_{t+1}), \sigma_t^2 I),
\]
for some choice of time-varying functions $f$, $b$, and $\sigma$. Writing down the divergence between the extended latent variable mappings, and using the data-processing inequality \parencite{cover1999elements}, we can show that the divergence between the extended mappings is an upper bound over \Cref{eq:divergence_loss},
\[
    \mathcal{D}_{KL}\left(q_{\phi}(x_0, \ldots, x_{T} \mid \mid p_{\theta}(x_0, \ldots, x_{T})\right) \geq \mathcal{D}\left( q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mu(z) \mid \mid p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \hat{\pi}(x) \right).
\]
As an aside, we note that we can write down the heirarchical mappings in the form of Markov chains with forward and backward transition densities defined through the forward and backward transition kernels $F_{\theta_t}$ and $B_{\phi_t}$ as
\begin{align}
    x_{t+1} \leftarrow x_{t} + f_{\theta_t}(x_t) + \sigma_t \epsilon_{t+1}, \quad F_{\theta_t}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t+1}; x_{t} + f_{\theta_t}(x_t), \sigma_t^2 I), \\
    x_{t} \leftarrow x_{t+1} + b_{\phi_t}(x_{t+1}) + \sigma_t \epsilon_{t}, \quad B_{\phi_t}(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t}; x_{t+1} + b_{\phi_t}(x_{t+1}), \sigma_t^2 I), \label{eq:framework_mc}
\end{align}
where $\{\epsilon_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ are i.i.d. standard normal random variables.

\nomenclature[z-sde]{$SDE$}{Stochastic Differential Equation}
\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: The Continuous Limit}
    \label{sec:continuous_limit}
    In the limit of infinite steps, where $T \to \infty$, the transition densities governing the mappings between latent variables $t$ and $t+1$ can be written down as the solutions to a pair of stochastic differential equations (SDEs)\footnote{This remark is a brief introduction to the continuous limit of the mappings $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$. We introduce the notation and terminology more rigorously in \Cref{chapter:sde}.} given by
    \[
        \mathrm{d}X_t = f_{\theta_t}(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_t \mathrm{d}W_t, \quad \mathrm{d}X_t = b_{\phi_t}(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_t \mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{W}_t, \label{eq:fwd_bwd_sde_sketch}
    \]
    where $\mathrm{d}W_t$ is the ``forward'' Wiener process, and $\mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{W}_t$ is the ``backward'' Wiener process. In this continuous limit, the joint distribution of the latent variables further converge to a quantity termed the ``path'' measure of the corresponding SDEs, which we refer to as $\rightP_{\theta}$ and $\leftP_{\phi}$ respectively. If we are able to define a valid probability divergence measure between the forward and backwards path measure, we can rewrite \Cref{eq:divergence_loss} in the continuous limit as
    \[
        \mathcal{L}_D(\theta, \phi) = \mathcal{L}_D(\rightP_{\theta} \| \leftP_{\phi}).
    \]
    As we will motivate in \Cref{chapter:sde}, it is non-trivial to easily define a valid probability divergence measure between the forward and backwards path measure, and as one of our main contributions, we will develop a unique change of measure between forward and backwards path measures that will enable us to use this framework to represent a large family of existing algorithms.
\end{lightgraybox}

Let us now discuss the individual paradigms of the sampling problems, as well as a few existing algorithms that have found great success and adoption in the machine learning community. Specifically, we choose to focus on algorithms that are readily amenable to the framework we have introduced, which is not necessarily a big restriction, as the algorithms we choose to focus on are some of the most popular algorithms in the field.

\section{Unnormalised Density Sampling}

\nomenclature[z-ebm]{$EBM$}{Energy-Based Model}

When a probability distribution $\pi$ is known only up to a normalisation constant, drawing samples from it is often non-trivial. Such distributions arise quite regularly in Boltzmann distributions such as in statistical physics and graphical models, and in modern energy-based models (EBMs). This issue is also quite prevalent in Bayesian inference applications where sampling from the posterior distribution is intractable due to an inability to estimate the normalising constant. To make our notation concrete, we write down $\pi$ in terms of the unnormalised probability density $\hpi$ and the normalising constant $Z$ as
\begin{equation}
    \pi = \frac{\hpi}{Z}, \quad Z = \int \hpi(x) \mathrm{d}x .
\end{equation}
A related problem is that of \textit{partition function estimation}, which is a special case of the problem of \textit{estimating expectations of functions} under the distribution $\pi$. We can write down the expectation of a function $f$ under $\pi$ as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{\pi} \left[ f(x) \right] = \frac{\int f(x) \hpi(x) \dd x}{\int \hpi(x) \dd x} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hpi} \left[ f(x) \right]}{Z}.
\end{equation}
and correspondingly, the partition function $Z$ can be written as
\begin{equation}
    Z = \int \hpi(x) \dd x = \mathbb{E}_{\hpi} \left[ \mathbb{I} \right]. \label{eq:partition_function}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Importance Sampling}

Importance Sampling (IS) algorithms tackle the problem of estimating expectations under $\pi$ by using a simpler approximating distribution $q$ that is easy to sample from. By a simple manipulation of the expectation, we can write
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E}_\pi[f] & =\frac{1}{Z} \int f(x) \hpi(x) \mathrm{d} x \\
    & = \frac{1}{Z} \int f(x) \frac{\hpi(x)}{q(x)} q(x) \mathrm{d} x \\
    & = \frac{1}{Z} \mathbb{E}_q[f(x) \underbrace{\frac{\hpi(x)}{q(x)}}_{w(x)}] \label{eq:is_weights}
\end{align}
Further, from Equation~\eqref{eq:partition_function}, we can see that the partition function $Z$ is simply the expectation of the indicator function under $\pi$, and so we can estimate $Z$ by drawing samples from $q$ and computing the empirical average of $w(x)$ over these samples, giving us
\begin{align}
    \hat{Z} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w(x^{(i)}), \quad x^{(i)} \sim q \label{eq:partition_function_estimate} \\ 
    \mathbb{E}_\pi[f] &\simeq \frac{1}{\hat{Z}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x^{(i)}) w(x^{(i)}) \label{eq:is_expectation_estimate}
\end{align}
Unfortunately, there are a few issues with this approach in general. Firstly, the choice of $q$ is critical to the performance of the algorithm, and in general, it is difficult to choose a $q$ that is both close to $\pi$ and easy to sample from. Secondly, IS techniques don't provide us with a way to design an approximation to $\pi$ that we can draw additional samples from, and we can merely use samples from $q$ to estimate expectations under $\pi$. We therefore turn our attention to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms, which can be used to sample from $\pi$ directly, and later, we see how we can consider an Importance Sampling viewpoint of MCMC algorithms that allow us to accurately estimate expectations under $\pi$.

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods allow us to construct a Markov chain with an equilibrium distribution that matches the target distribution $\pi$. While the literature of MCMC algorithms is vast, and we could dedicate an entire chapter to summarising the recent advances, for the purpose of this thesis, we will focus on one specific algorithm: Langevin dynamics, which can exploit information about the gradient of the log-density of $\hpi$ to obtain high-quality samples compared to other MCMC variants. Specifically, assuming a sample from a simple distribution $x_0 \sim \mu$, we can follow the following iterative procedure
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1} \leftarrow x_t + \Delta t \nabla_x \log \hpi(x) + \sqrt{2 \Delta t} \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \quad t = 0, \ldots, T \label{eq:langevin_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ is a small interval. When $\Delta t \rightarrow 0$ and $T \rightarrow \infty$, it can be shown that $x_T$ obtained by the procedure above converges to a sample from $\pi(x)$ under some regularity conditions.

Vanilla Langevin dynamics can struggle in practise to produce samples that are diverse over the entire support of $\hpi$, as we use the gradient of the log-density of $\hpi$ to iteratively update the sample, and this can result in the samples being trapped in local modes of the density. We see next approaches that tackle this issue.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of Langevin Dynamics}
In fact, our choice of using $t$ to refer to the discrete index foreshadows what Langevin dynamics converges to in the continuous limit: a time-varying stochastic differential equation. Intuitively, we could write down a differential equation of $x$ w.r.t $t$ as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}x_t}{\mathrm{d}t} = \nabla_x \log \hpi(x_t) + C \times \frac{\mathrm{d} \text{``noise''} }{\mathrm{d}t}, \label{eq:sde_intuitive}
\end{equation}
for some constant $C$. The introduction of a noise term in the differential equation introduces subtle issues that prevent us from relying on traditional ordinary differential equation machinery, and we will need to develop a ``stochastic'' calculus framework in order to successfully find solutions for equations of such form. However, here, it suffices to say that Langevin dynamics is a specific instantiation of an SDE.

\end{lightgraybox}

\nomenclature[z-sa]{$SA$}{Simulated Annealing}
Simulated Annealing (SA) \parencite{kirkpatrick1983optimization} was introduced as a way to improve the mixing/coverage of MCMC sampling. Instead of designing a Markov Chain that directly samples from 
$\pi = \pi_T$, we design a Markov Chain that samples from a sequence of distributions $\pi_0, \pi_1, \ldots, \pi_T$ that interpolate between a simple distribution $\pi_0$ and the target distribution $\pi_T$. We first run a Markov Chain designed to sample from $\pi_0$ for a number of steps (usually less than required for equilibrium). Using these samples at a starting point, we then simulate a Markov Chain for $\pi_1$, and so on. $\pi_0$ is usually designed to have really good coverage over the space where different modes for $\pi$ might exist, allowing for good mixing.

While we can obtain samples covering multiple modes using SA, there are unfortunately no guarantees on obtaining correct results (i.e. each mode of $\pi$ is represented with the correct probability), or estimating partition functions and expectations in a variance-reduced way. In order to solve these issues, we turn to another method that views this procedure as an importance sampling problem.

\subsection{Annealed Importance Sampling}

Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) \parencite{neal2001annealed,jarzynski1997equilibrium,jarzynski1997nonequilibrium} was proposed independently by Radford Neal for statistical applications, as well as by Christopher Jarzynski for estimating free energies. Effectively, Radford Neal provided an importance sampling viewpoint of annealing, which allows us to assign weights to the samples obtained from different annealing runs, and use these weights to converge to the correct distribution as the number of runs increases. As before, AIS introduces a series of intermediate distributions starting from a simple distribution $\pi_0 := \mu$ until the distribution of interest $\pi_T := \pi$. 

We first define a sequence of unnormalised distributions $\hpi_t(x)$ that are proportional to the annealed distributions $\pi_t(x)$, with $\hpi_T(x)=\hpi(x)$. Second, for each intermediate distribution $\hpi_t$, we require that we can simulate a Markov Chain using a forward transition density $F_t$ that has $\pi_t$ as its equilibrium distribution. Given these two design choices (of $\hpi_t$ and $F_t$), the algorithm proceeds by first sampling $x_0 \sim \pi_0$, then simulating a Markov chain using $F_t$ for $t=1$ with $x_0$ as the starting point to obtain $x_1$, and so on until $T$. Usually, we run the chain at each step $t$ for a fixed number of steps, and then use the final sample $x_T$ as the sample from $\pi$. 

In order to calculate the importance sampling weights for the $T$-th distribution, we would need to calculate $w_{\text {mar }}\left(x_T\right)=\hat{\pi}\left(x_T\right) / q_T\left(x_T\right)$. In order to do this, we first consider the extended space defined over the path that AIS takes, i.e. jointly over the points $\left(x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_T\right)$. The effective proposal distribution over this extended space is given by
\begin{equation}
    Q\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\pi_0\left(x_0\right) \prod_{t=1}^T F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right).
\end{equation}
Therefore, the marginal proposal distributions at any intermediate step $q_t$ would be defined as
\begin{equation}
    q_t\left(x_t\right)=\int q_{t-1}\left(x_{t-1}\right) F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1} \text { for } t \geq 1, \quad q_0\left(x_0\right)=\pi_0\left(x_0\right).
\end{equation}
Unfortunately, we see that this quantity is typically intractable for $t \geq 1$, which in turn makes it intractable to calculate the importance sampling weights $w_{\text{mar}}(x_T)$ for the $T$-th distribution. 

In AIS, instead of attempting to calculate the importance sampling distribution corresponding to our original target distribution $\hpi$, we instead consider defining an auxilliary target distribution over the extended space as follows
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\hat{\pi}\left(x_T\right) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} B_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t+1}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\left(B_t\right)_{t=0}^{T-1}$ are corresponding backwards Markov transition kernels that are invariant to $\pi_t$ and transform intermediate samples $x_t$ to $x_{t-1}$. For this extended space, it is straightforward to calculate the importance sampling weights similar to Equation~\eqref{eq:is_weights} as
\begin{equation}
    w\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(x_{0: T}\right)}{Q\left(x_{0: T}\right)}. \label{eq:ais_weights}
\end{equation}
These weights can be computed efficiently in an iterative manner while proceeding through the sampling procedure, as we see in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ais}. Using these samples $x_0^{(i)}, \ldots, x_T^{(i)}$ with their corresponding weights $w^{(i)}$, we can estimate expectations with Equation~\eqref{eq:is_expectation_estimate} and the partition function with Equation~\eqref{eq:partition_function_estimate}.

The AIS algorithm is quite flexible, and allows us to make a few choices in the design of the intermediate distributions $\left(\hpi_t\right)_{t=0}^T$ and the forward and backward transition kernels $\left(F_t\right)_{t=1}^T$ and $\left(B_t\right)_{t=0}^{T-1}$. For example, we can pick suitable forward transition kernels $\left(F_t\right)_{t=1}^T$ as long as they are invariant to the annealed distribution $\pi_t$, and common choices include MCMC kernels (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs', etc), Langevin dynamics, etc. Correspondingly, we can pick backwards transition kernels $\left(B_t\right)_{t=0}^{T-1}$ with properties beneficial to us. In fact, modern extensions of AIS allow for parametrised choices of $F_t$ and $B_t$ that can reduce the variance of the importance sampling weights, or be learnable through backpropagation, bringing them closer to our framework discussed in \Cref{eq:framework_mc}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Sequence of unnormalized distributions $\{\hat{\pi}_t\}_{t=0}^T$ with $\hat{\pi}_T = \hat{\pi}$
\REQUIRE Forward transition kernels $\{F_t\}_{t=1}^T$ with $F_t$ invariant to $\pi_t$
\STATE Sample $x_0^{(i)} \sim \pi_0(x)$ from initial distribution
\STATE Initialize weight $w^{(i)} = 1$
\FOR{$t = 1$ to $T$}
    \STATE Sample $x_t^{(i)} \sim F_t(x_t | x_{t-1}^{(i)})$ from forward transition
    \STATE Update weight $w^{(i)} \leftarrow w^{(i)} \cdot \frac{\hat{\pi}_t(x_{t-1}^{(i)})}{\hat{\pi}_{t-1}(x_{t-1}^{(i)})}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Set $x^{(i)} = x_T^{(i)}$ as final sample
\RETURN $\{(x^{(i)}, w^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N$
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS)}
\label{alg:ais}
\end{algorithm}

In \textcite{neal2001annealed}, Radford Neal chose a Metropolis-Hastings sampler along with a specific choice of backwards transition kernel given by
\begin{equation}
    B_t^{\text {ais }}\left(x_t \mid x_{t+1}\right)=\frac{\hpi_{t+1}\left(x_t\right) F_{t+1}\left(x_{t+1} \mid x_t\right)}{\hpi_{t+1}\left(x_{t+1}\right)},
\end{equation}

which simplifies the calculation of the importance sampling weights in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ais}. Another closely-related algorithm is the Unadjusted Langevin Annealing (ULA) sampler \parencite{wu2020stochastic,welling2011bayesian,thin2021monte}, which defines the forward transition kernel as an approximate Langevin dynamics algorithm that uses intermediate annealed distributions at each time step (instead of the target distribution itself, as defined in \Cref{eq:langevin_dynamics})
\[
    x_{t+1} \leftarrow x_t + \Delta t \nabla_x \log \hpi_t(x) + \sqrt{2 \Delta t} \epsilon_i, \quad \epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \quad t = 0, \ldots, T.
\]
Furthermore, the backwards kernel is defined quite simply as the reverse of the forward kernel, i.e. $B_t^{\text {ula }}(x_t \mid x_{t+1})=F_{t+1}(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)$.
% NOTE: Check if time index is correct?

\nomenclature[z-ula]{$ULA$}{Unadjusted Langevin Annealing}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of AIS}
    We can see that the framework of AIS simplifies to the choice of a forward Markov Chain from our proposal $\mu$ to the target $\hpi$ parametrised by a forward transition kernel $F_{\theta_t}$, and a backwards Markov Chain similarly parametrised by a backward transition kernel $B_{\phi_t}$. These Markov chains converge to forwards and backwards SDEs in the continuous-time limit, similar to \Cref{eq:fwd_bwd_sde_sketch}. Furthermore, the effective proposal and target distributions converge to the corresponding path measures of the SDEs
    \[
        Q(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \rightP_{\theta}, \quad \Gamma(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \leftP_{\phi} \label{eq:ais_path_measures}
    \]
    Similarly, the importance sampling weights in \Cref{eq:ais_weights} converge to the density ratio between the path measures in \Cref{eq:ais_path_measures}, which in turn is dfined as a quantity called the Radon-Nikodym derivative
    \[
        w(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \frac{\rightP_{\theta}(x_{0:T})}{\leftP_{\phi}(x_{0:T})} \rightarrow \frac{\dd \rightP_{\theta}}{\dd \leftP_{\phi}}
    \]
    We will see that the Radon-Nikodym derivative is not well-defined for the density ratio of a forward path measure w.r.t a backward path measure, and we will develop the theoretical framework to define this novel quantity in \Cref{chapter:cmcd}.
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Generative Modelling}

In certain situations, we don't have access to the explicit form of $\hpi$, or the ability to take gradients of the density. Instead, we have access to a collection of samples from the density through a training dataset $\Dtrain = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}$. We wish to learn a parametrised approximation $\pi_{\theta}$\footnote{For notational clarity, we refer to the parametrised marginal probability distribution over $x$ with $\pi_\theta$, and the corresponding parametrised conditional probability distribution mapping $z$ to $x$ as $p_\theta$.} to the density of interest $\hpi$ for two goals: to draw new, novel samples from $\hpi$ and optionally, to be able to evaluate $\hpi$ on existing samples. This task is commonly called generative modeling, as we attempt to "generate" new samples from our unknown density by first "modeling" it through an existing training dataset. This paradigm has found immense success in the modern deep learning era, ranging from image synthesis to language modelling, as well as in scientific domains such as protein structure prediction and drug design.

In terms of our framework, many algorithms find it an easier task to first learn a mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ from training samples $x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ to a latent space $z$ that has an easy-to-sample distribution $\mu$. Given this mapping, many algorithms focus on different approaches to learn the "reverse" mapping, i.e. $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$, allowing us to generate novel samples by sampling from $\mu$ and mapping it to the data distribution $\hat{\pi}$ through $q_{\phi}$. The specific design choices of $p_{\theta}$ and $q_{\phi}$ cover a large family of algorithms, which we briefly discuss below. For the sake of completeness, we first discuss Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs) \parencite{kingma2019introduction} as a flexible algorithm that follows our framework quite closely, albeit without a heirarchical structure by default. We then turn our attention to a large collection of algorithms that focus on designing more flexible mappings using heirarchical latent variables (whether implicitly or explicitly), such as Normalising Flows (NFs) \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}, Score Matching (SM) \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score} and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) \parencite{ho2020denoising}.

\subsection{Variational Auto-Encoders}

\nomenclature[z-vae]{$VAE$}{Variational Auto-Encoder}

VAEs parametrise $p_{\theta}$ (termed the \textit{decoder}) from the latent space $z$ to the data $x$ with a neural network, allowing us to map individual datapoints from $x^{(i)} \sim \Dtrain$ to corresponding latent variables $z^{(i)}$. The issue arises when we attempt to recover an $x$ corresponding to a sample $z \sim \mu$, as $p(z \mid x)$ is intractable due to the integral involved in the marginal likelihood term $p_{\theta}(x)$, as can be seen from Bayes' rule
\begin{equation}
    p(z \mid x) = \frac{p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z)}{p_{\theta}(x)}, \quad p_{\theta}(x) = \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z) \dd z
\end{equation}
Instead, a VAE learns a corresponding \textit{encoder} model $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ to approximate $p(z \mid x)$. Here, we follow the somewhat unconventional derivation of \textcite{kingma2019introduction} to derive the loss function for a VAE as a lower bound to the marginal log-likelihood, which is given as
\begin{align}
    \log p_{\phi}(x) &= \E_{q_{\theta}(z \mid x)} \left[ \log p_{\phi}(x) \right] \\
    &= \underbrace{\E_{q_{\theta}(z \mid x)} \left[ \log \left[ \frac{p_{\phi}(x, z)}{q_{\theta}(z \mid x)} \right] \right]}_{\substack{=\mathcal{L}_{\theta, \phi}(x) \\(\mathrm{ELBO})}} - \underbrace{\E_{q_{\theta}(z \mid x)} \left[ \log \left[ \frac{q_{\theta}(z \mid x)}{p_{\phi}(z \mid x)} \right] \right]}_{D_{KL}(q_{\theta}(z \mid x) \mid \mid p_{\phi}(z \mid x))} \label{eq:vae_loss}
\end{align}
% NOTE: Show more clearly how this is just KL-divergence between p_theta and q_phi.
Since the KL-divergence term in \Cref{eq:vae_loss} is non-negative, we can see that the ELBO is a lower bound to the marginal log-likelihood, and in an ideal setting, one learns the parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$ by maximising the ELBO over the training dataset $\Dtrain$. In practice, VAEs often struggle to learn a sufficiently flexible encoder $q_{\theta}(z \mid x)$ and decoder $p_{\phi}(x \mid z)$, resulting in poor (or blurry) samples from the model. Intuitively, we can see how VAEs lie within our framework, albeit without using intermediate latent variables to parametrise the mapping. However, by allowing a more heirarchical structure, we can design more flexible models that can learn more complex mappings, which we will see in the next section.

\subsection{Normalising Flows}

Normalising flows learn the mapping from $x$ to $z$ using a series of composable invertible parametrised transformations $x = f_{\theta}(z)$, which provides the reverse mapping quite efficiently as the inverse of the function, giving us $z = f_{\theta}^{-1}(x)$. Then, using the change of variables formula, we can write the probability distribution of $x$ as
\begin{equation}
    p_{\theta}(x \mid z) = \mu (z) \left| \det \nabla_{z} f_{\theta}^{-1}(z) \right|.
\end{equation}
Normalising flows tend to perform really well in practise, owing to the flexibility of composing a series of iterative transfomations to obtain a more complex mapping. However, the flexibility of these transformations is still limited by the requirements of invertibility and differentiability, which are often difficult to satisfy. As we see later, the idea of iterative mappings are central to the design of models we consider, and future methods have focused on designing more flexible transformations.

\subsection{Score Matching}
\label{sec:sm}

Energy-based approaches (such as EBMs) learn a parametrised representation of $\hat{\pi}$, and run into the issue of having to estimate the intractable normalising constant. On the other hand, Score Matching (SM) \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score} attempts to learn a different quantity, the "score" of the probability distribution. This quantity is defined as the gradient of the logarithm of the probability distribution, which gets rid of the necessity of learning the normalising constant,
\begin{equation}
    s_{\theta}(x) \simeq \nabla_x \log \hat{\pi}(x) = - \nabla_x f_{\theta}(x) - \underbrace{\nabla_x Z_{\theta}}_{0}.
\end{equation}
Vanilla score-matching models are usually learnt by minimising the Fisher divergence between the parametrised score function and the actual quantity of interest, giving us 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{VSM}} = \mathbb{E}_{\hat{\pi}} \left[ \| \nabla_x \log \hat{\pi} (x) - s_{\theta}(x) \|_2^2 \right].
\end{equation}
Due to the structure of the loss function taking an expectation over samples from $\hat{\pi}$, vanilla score-matching techniques often really struggle in approximating $\hat{\pi}$ in regions of low probability where samples are sparse. In order to address this issue, the authors resort to a technique that we will see repeated quite often in the current landscape: \textit{iterative noising}. Instead of learning a score model for $\hat{\pi}$ directly, score-matching creates noisier and noisier versions of $\hat{\pi}$ by adding successively larger amounts of Gaussian noise, and learns a score model per level of noise. If we assume a schedule of noise values $\sigma_1 \leq \ldots \leq \sigma_T$, we can write down each noised distribution as 
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{t}(x) = \int \hat{\pi} (y) \mathcal{N}(x; y, \sigma_t^2) \dd y, \quad t = 1, \ldots, T.
\end{equation}
We can then modify our score model to learn a different parametrisation for a different noise scale, resulting in a Noise-Conditional Score Network (NCSN) given by $s_{\theta}(x, t)$ and a weighted sum of Fisher divergences per noise scale, given as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\sigma_t}} \left[ \| \nabla_x \log \pi_{\sigma_t} (x) - s_{\theta}(x, t) \|_2^2 \right]. \label{eq:sm_loss}
\end{equation}
While score-matching was initially motivated by this heuristic argument about learning the score of a multi-level noised distribution, in \textcite{song2020score}, the authors show that the noising process can be represented as a stochastic differential equation that iteratively noises samples from the data distribution $\hpi$ until they resemble Gaussian noise (i.e. $\mu$).

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of Score-Matching}
    As shown in \textcite{song2020score}, the iterative noising process can be represented as a stochastic differential equation in the continuous limit. Here, we redefine our notation to follow standard notation in the literature closely, and assume that the distribution at $t=0$ corresponds to the data distribution $\hpi$, and the distribution at $t=T$ corresponds to the base distribution $\mu$. In this case, the iterative noising process can be represented as a forward SDE, transporting samples from $\hpi$ to $\mu$, and is given by 
    \[
        \dd X_t = \sqrt{\frac{\dd \sigma_t^2}{\dd t}} \dd W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \hpi, \quad t = 0, \ldots, T, \label{eq:sm_sde_fwd}
    \]
    where $W_t$ is a Wiener process\footnote{We define these processes more rigorously in \Cref{chapter:sde}.}, and with corresponding path measure $\rightP_{\phi}$, where $\phi = \{\sigma_t\}$. As we will see in \Cref{chapter:sde}, it is possible to define a ``reversal'' of an SDE that shares the same marginal probability distributions as the original SDE, but travels in the opposite direction. In this case, the ``reversal`` of the backward SDE in \Cref{eq:sm_sde_fwd} will be a backward SDE that transports the base distribution $\mu$ to the data distribution $\hpi$, is parametrised by the variable $\theta$ through the inclusion of a score term in backward drift, and has a corresponding path measure $\rightP_{\theta}$. As we will show more concretely in \Cref{chapter:cmcd}, the loss function for score-matching as defined in \Cref{eq:sm_loss} converges to minimising the KL divergence between the path measures $\rightP_{\phi}$ and $\leftP_{\theta}$.
\end{lightgraybox}

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models}
\label{sec:ddpm}

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) \parencite{ho2020denoising} are another popular framework for generative modelling using the notion of ``iterative noising'' that have found massive success. DDPM imposes additional structure on the mapping from data to latent $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ by introducing a series of additional latent variables $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}\}$ with $x_0 := x$ and $x_T := z$\footnote{We note here that this convention is the opposite of that considered by sampling techniques such as AIS, but consistent with the notation chosen by Score Matching algorithms, as can be seen from \Cref{eq:sm_sde_fwd}.}, and assuming a Markov assumption on the chain of latents, such that $x_t$ given $x_{t-1}$ is independent of previous latents. The authors then specifically choose a form of "noising" process that iteratively adds Gaussian noise to each latent at time $t-1$ to obtain the latent at time $t$, giving us the noising distributions
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t ; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I), \label{eq:ddpm_noising}
\end{equation}
for some choice of noising $\beta_t$ schedule. Due to each individual noising process being Gaussian, a nice property of the DDPM formulation is that we can represent the latent at any time step $t$ w.r.t the original data sample $x = x_0$ in closed-form\footnote{See Appendix A for a proof.}, giving us
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_t ; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t I)\right), \quad \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{t=1}^T \alpha_t\text{ with } \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t.
\end{equation}
If we could now learn a reverse "denoising" process $p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ that takes a sample from the noising distribution at time $t$ and maps it back to the original data distribution iteratively, we could sample from the original data distribution by starting with a sample from the base distribution $x_T \sim \pi_0$ and iteratively applying the reverse noising process. However, calculating the exact reverse noising process is intractable, as we see here
\begin{align}
    p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) &= \frac{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) p(x_{t-1})}{p(x_t)} \\
    &= \frac{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \int q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0) \pi_0(x_0) \dd x_0}{\int q(x_t \mid x_0) \pi_0(x_0) \dd x_0},
\end{align}
where the integrals over the data distribution $\pi_0$ are intractable. Instead, DDPM assumes a variational distribution $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_{\theta}(x_t, t), \Sigma_{\theta}(x_t, t))$ that is a Gaussian with mean and variance parametrised by a neural network, and learns the parameters $\theta$ in the same way as a VAE (where the latents $z = \{x_1, \ldots, x_{T}\}$). We can therefore write down an ELBO similar to \Cref{eq:vae_loss}, giving us
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}} = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \left[ \log \left[ \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right] \right].
\end{equation}
Interestingly, \textcite{song2020score} showed that in the continuous limit, the iterative noising process for DDPM converges to an SDE (different from the SDE in Score Matching), and therefore both Score Matching and DDPM are variants of a score-matching loss, albeit on different forward SDEs.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of DDPM}
    As shown in \textcite{song2020score}, the iterative noising process in \Cref{eq:ddpm_noising} can be represented as a stochastic differential equation in the continuous limit. Making the same assumptions as Score Matching ($t=0$ corresponds to the data distribution $\hpi$ and $t=T$ corresponds to the base distribution $\mu$), the forward SDE is given by
    \[
        \dd X_t = -\frac{1}{2} \beta_t X_t \dd t + \sqrt{\beta_t} \dd W_t.
    \]
    The DDPM objective, which is equivalent to the Score Matching objective \parencite{song2020score}, can therefore be written in terms of the KL divergence between the forward and backward path measures.
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Conditional Sampling}

In the conditional sampling framework, we often assume one of two situations. First, in a generative modelling setting, we have access to the unconditional distribution $\hpi$ through the mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$, and we wish to instead learn a mapping to a modified distribution $\piy$ that is a "tilted" or "conditioned" version of $\hpi$ based on some additional constraints. We can write this additional constraint as either a generic reward function $r(x)$ that results in $\piy \propto \hpi \exp(r(x))$, or an explicit likelihood model $p(y \mid x)$ that results in $\piy \propto \hpi p(y \mid x)$. In this situation, we attempt to learn a conditional mapping $p_{\cdot \mid y}(x \mid y, z)$ that modifies the original mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ based on a dataset of datapoints and conditions $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cond}} = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cond}}}$. This approach is termed \textit{conditional generative modelling}, as we eventually learn a way to map from a latent space to the conditioned distribution, often with the (somewhat strong) assumption that we have a-priori access to a map to the unconditioned distribution. Similar to our previous discussions, many cases of generative modeling algorithms that converge to stochastic differential equations allow for additional conditioning that also has a continuous time perspective in the SDE regime. In fact, a large number of baseline algorithms that modify existing iterative noising generative modeling algorithms such as Score Matching (Section~\ref{sec:sm}) and DDPM (Section~\ref{sec:ddpm}) are easier to understand with the SDE perspective, and so we defer their discussion until the next Chapter.

The second situation arises in more specific situations where we have the ability to efficiently draw samples from the unconditional distribution $\hpi$, and we wish to transform these samples by modifying them to instead have been generated from the conditioned distribution $\piy$. This approach is called \textit{pathwise conditioning}. There are often drawbacks in this situation: we may not be able to learn an arbitrary map that can map latent draws to samples from the conditional distribution, and only be able to convert samples from the unconditional distribution to ones from the conditional distribution. Also, this is usually computationally efficient only when drawing samples from the unconditional distribution are feasible or efficient in the first place, a situation that is quite rare in high-dimensional distributions. A very useful family of distributions that are quite appealing given this framework are Gaussian distributions, where it is often easy to sample from the unconditional distribution, and under specific likelihood models, the conditional distribution is also Gaussian, allowing for closed-form calculation of the correction factor using Matheron's rule. In the limit of Gaussian distributions on an infinite number of datapoints, we converge to a special case of stochastic process termed a Gaussian Process (GP). In the second half of this thesis, we will extensively develop the theory of GPs, and show how they shine in particular applications where uncertainty estimation is valuable: Bayesian inference. We will show how many Bayesian inference problems in the deep learning setting can be simplified to GPs, and therefore GPs provide a powerful, unifying framework for a scalable, flexible, and high-performance Bayesian inference framework.

In the first situation, we usually assume access to a parametric mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ between the latent space and the unconditional distribution, and algorithms attempt to "correct" or "guide" this mapping to the conditional mapping. This family of algorithms cover approaches such as classifier(-free) guidance, Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS), and Red-DIFF, among others, and can be considered a \textit{distributional approach}. The second family of algorithms merely assume access to samples from the prior distribution, and transform individual samples to those that would satisfy the law of the conditional distribution. We term this a \textit{sample-based approach}.
