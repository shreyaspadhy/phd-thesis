\vspace{-0.3cm}\Cref{chapter:introduction} has highlighted the critical role of sampling from complex, high-dimensional probability distributions across modern machine learning, from generative modelling to large-scale Bayesian inference, and outlined this thesis's core contributions towards unifying frameworks and scalable algorithms. Before delving into our novel methodologies in subsequent chapters, this chapter provides a foundational overview of the sampling problem itself, establishing common terminology and reviewing key existing approaches. Further, we attempt to show how many existing algorithms are specific instantiations of an underlying stochastic differential equation, therefore motivating stochastic calculus as a building block for our unifying frameworks.

\label{ntn:mapping_x_to_z}\label{ntn:base_distbn}
The task of sampling in machine learning can be broadly divided into three distinct paradigms. In the first task of \emph{unnormalised density sampling}, (often termed simply \emph{sampling}), we can evaluate an unnormalised density $\hpi(x)$ and typically its gradient\footnote{The assumption is often made that one can evaluate the gradient of the log-density $\nabla_x \ln \hpi$. These are equivalent assumptions, as by the chain rule of calculus we can write $\nabla_x \ln \hpi(x) = \frac{1}{\hpi(x)} \nabla_x \hpi(x)$.} $\nabla_x \hpi$, but lack access to direct samples from $\hpi(x)$. The objective then is to generate such samples to approximate expectations or estimate normalising constants, often by learning a parametrised mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ that transforms samples from a simple base distribution $\mu(z)$ to samples from $\hpi(x)$ (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, (I.)).

% In the first situation, we don't have any existing samples from an unnormalised probability distribution, and we are only able to evaluate the unnormalised density $\hpi(x)$ and the gradient of the density $\nabla_x \hpi(x)$The task of \textit{unnormalised density sampling} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, I.) focuses on learning to draw samples from this probability distribution (and often-times using these samples to estimate the normalising constant). 
\label{ntn:generative_modelling}\label{ntn:training_dataset}
Secondly, \textit{generative modelling} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, (II.)) addresses scenarios where we possess a dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}$ drawn from $\pi(x)$, but cannot directly evaluate $\pi(x)$ or its gradients. The goal is then to learn a parametrised approximation $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ that approximates $\pi(x)$, enabling the generation of new samples by transforming samples from a base distribution $\mu(z)$ (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, (II.)).

% The task therefore focuses on using these samples to learn parametrised approximations to our probability distribution of interest, allowing us to draw novel samples from this approximation, and sometimes allowing us to estimate the density $\hpi(x)$. 
\label{ntn:conditional_dataset}\label{ntn:conditional_prob_distbn}
A third crucial task, \emph{conditional sampling}, arises when we aim to draw samples from a \emph{conditioned} distribution $\pi_{\text{cond}} := \pi(x \mid y)$ that is contingent upon some observation or context $y$. This is central to many applications, including Bayesian inference where $\pi(x \mid y) \propto p(y \mid x) \pi(x)$ represents the posterior distribution given a prior $\pi(x)$ and likelihood $p(y \mid x)$. Within conditional sampling, we can further distinguish two primary approaches. The first, \emph{conditional generative modelling}
(\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, (III.A)), typically assumes an existing (or jointly learned) model for the unconditional data distribution $\pi(x)$ (or $p_\theta(x \mid z)$ ) and seeks to learn a parametric model $p_{\theta, \text{cond}}(x \mid y, z)$ for $\pi_{\text{cond}}$, often leveraging paired data $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cond}} = \{x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cond}}}$. The second approach, which we term \emph{pathwise conditioning} (\Cref{fig:sampling_paradigm}, (III.B)), focuses on directly transforming samples from the unconditional distribution $\pi(x)$ into samples from $\pi_{\text{cond}}$. This is often feasible when $\pi(x)$ and $p(y \mid x)$ possess tractable forms, such as Gaussian distributions, allowing for an analytical modification of individual sample paths. 

This chapter will review foundational techniques within each of these paradigms, setting the stage for the unifying frameworks and novel algorithms developed later in this thesis. We discuss these paradigms in turn.

\section{Unnormalised Density Sampling}
\label{sec:unnormalised_density_sampling}
\nomenclature[z-ebm]{$EBM$}{Energy-Based Model}

When a probability distribution $\pi$ is known only up to a normalisation constant, drawing samples from it is often non-trivial. Such distributions arise quite regularly in Boltzmann distributions \citep{leimkuhler2015molecular}, statistical physics \citep{ferraccioli2021nonparametric}, graphical models \citep{lin2016estimation}, and in modern energy-based models (EBMs) \citep{song2021train}. This issue is also quite prevalent in Bayesian inference applications where sampling from the posterior distribution is intractable due to an inability to estimate the normalising constant \citep{izmailov2021bayesian}. We write down $\pi$ in terms of the unnormalised probability density $\hpi$ and the normalising constant $Z$ \label{ntn:normalising_constant} as
\[
    \pi = \frac{\hpi}{Z}, \quad Z = \int \hpi(x) \mathrm{d}x .
\]
A related problem is that of \textit{partition function estimation}, which is a special case of the problem of \textit{estimating expectations of functions} under the distribution $\pi$. We can write down the expectation of a function $f$ under $\pi$, involving $Z$, as
\[
    \mathbb{E}_{\pi} \left[ f(x) \right] = \frac{\int f(x) \hpi(x) \dd x}{\int \hpi(x) \dd x} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hpi} \left[ f(x) \right]}{Z} = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{\hpi} \left[ f(x) \right]}{\mathbb{E}_{\hpi} \left[ 1 \right]}.  \label{eq:partition_function}
\]

Importance Sampling (IS) algorithms \citep{kloek1978bayesian}tackle the problem of estimating expectations under $\pi$ by using a simpler approximating distribution $q$ that is easy to sample from. By a simple manipulation of the expectation, we can write
\[
    \mathbb{E}_\pi[f] = \frac{1}{Z} \int f(x) \frac{\hpi(x)}{q(x)} q(x) \mathrm{d} x = \frac{1}{Z} \mathbb{E}_q[f(x) \underbrace{\frac{\hpi(x)}{q(x)}}_{w(x)}]. \label{eq:is_weights}
\]
Consequently, we can write expectations under $\hpi$ as expectations under $q$,\label{ntn:importance_dist} i.e. $\mathbb{E}_{\hpi}[f] = \mathbb{E}_q[f(x) w(x)]$. From \Cref{eq:partition_function}, we see that $Z$ is simply the expectation of the indicator function under $\hpi$, and so we can estimate $Z$ by drawing samples from $q$ and computing the empirical average of $w(x)$ over these samples, giving us the empirical estimate $\hat{Z}$ as:\label{ntn:importance_weights}\label{ntn:importance_weights_estimate}
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \hat{Z} &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} w(x^{(i)}), \quad x^{(i)} \sim q \label{eq:partition_function_estimate}, \\ 
    \mathbb{E}_\pi[f] &\approx \frac{1}{\hat{Z}} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x^{(i)}) w(x^{(i)}) \label{eq:is_expectation_estimate}.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Unfortunately, IS approaches run into some issues. Firstly, the choice of $q$ is critical to the performance of the algorithm, and in general, it is difficult to choose a $q$ that has good coverage over the support of $\pi$ and is easy to sample from. Secondly, this approach doesn't provide us with a way to design an approximation to $\pi$ that we can draw additional samples from; we can merely estimate expectations. We therefore turn our attention to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms, which can be used to draw samples from $\pi$ directly, and later, we see how we can consider an Importance Sampling viewpoint of MCMC algorithms that allow us to accurately estimate expectations as well.

\subsection{MCMC Algorithms and Langevin Dynamics}
\label{sec:mcmc}

Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods \citep{andrieu2003introduction,betancourt2017conceptual} allow us to construct a Markov chain with an equilibrium distribution that matches the target distribution $\pi$. While the literature of MCMC algorithms is vast, and we could dedicate an entire chapter to summarising the recent advances, for the purpose of this thesis, we will focus on one specific algorithm: Langevin dynamics, which can exploit information about the gradient of the log-density of $\hpi$ to obtain high-quality samples compared to other MCMC variants \citep{roberts1996exponential}. Specifically, assuming a sample from a simple distribution $x_0 \sim \mu$, we can follow the following iterative procedure
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1} \leftarrow x_t + \Delta t \nabla_x \ln \hpi(x) + \sqrt{2 \Delta t} \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_{t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \quad t = 0, \ldots, T \label{eq:langevin_dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ is a small interval. When $\Delta t \rightarrow 0$ and $T \rightarrow \infty$, it can be shown that $x_T$ obtained by the procedure above converges to a sample from $\pi(x)$ under some regularity conditions.

Vanilla Langevin dynamics can struggle in practise to produce samples that are diverse over the entire support of $\hpi$, as we use the gradient of the log-density of $\hpi$ to iteratively update the sample, and this can result in the samples being trapped in local modes of the density. Next, we see approaches that tackle this issue.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of Langevin Dynamics}
In fact, our choice of using $t$ to refer to the discrete index foreshadows what Langevin dynamics converges to in the continuous limit: a time-varying stochastic differential equation. Intuitively, we could write down \Cref{eq:langevin_dynamics} as a differential equation of $x$ w.r.t $t$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}x_t}{\mathrm{d}t} = \nabla_x \ln \hpi(x_t) + C \times \frac{\mathrm{d} \text{``noise''} }{\mathrm{d}t}, \label{eq:sde_intuitive}
\end{equation}
for some constant $C$. The introduction of a noise term in the differential equation introduces subtle issues that prevent us from relying on traditional ordinary differential equation machinery, and we will need to develop a \emph{stochastic} calculus framework in order to successfully find solutions for equations of such form. Here, however, it suffices to say that Langevin dynamics is a specific instantiation of an SDE.
\end{lightgraybox}

\nomenclature[z-sa]{$SA$}{Simulated Annealing}
Simulated Annealing (SA) \parencite{kirkpatrick1983optimization} was introduced as a way to improve the mixing and coverage of MCMC samplers. Instead of designing a Markov Chain that directly samples from 
$\pi$, we design a series of Markov Chains that sample from a sequence of distributions $\{\pi_0, \pi_1, \ldots, \pi_T\}$ that interpolate between a simple distribution $\pi_0:=\mu$ \label{ntn:annealing_dists} and the target distribution $\pi_T:=\pi$. In practise, each Markov chain for $\pi_t$ is run for fewer steps than needed for equilibrium, and the samples from the previous chain are used as initialisations for the next. $\pi_0$ is usually designed to have really good coverage over the space where different modes for $\pi$ might exist, allowing for good mixing.

While we can obtain samples covering multiple modes using SA, there are unfortunately no guarantees on obtaining accurate expectations and normalising constants--i.e. \emph{is each mode of $\pi$ is represented with the correct probability?}. In order to solve these issues, we turn to a method that views this procedure as an importance sampling problem.

\subsection{Annealed Importance Sampling}
\label{sec:ais}
Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) \parencite{neal2001annealed,jarzynski1997equilibrium,jarzynski1997nonequilibrium} proposed independently by Radford Neal for statistical applications and Christopher Jarzynski for estimating free energies, offers an importance sampling perspective on simulated annealing. This allows for the assignment of importance weights to samples obtained from annealing runs, enabling convergence to the target distribution as the number of runs increases. 
% As before, AIS introduces a series of intermediate distributions starting from a simple distribution $\pi_0 := \mu$ until the distribution of interest $\pi_T := \pi$. 
The method involves defining a sequence of distributions $\{\pi_t(x)\}_{t=0}^T$, where $\pi_T := \pi$ is the target. For each intermediate distribution $\pi_t$, a forward Markov transition kernel $F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right)$ is chosen such that $F_t$ leaves $\pi_t$ invariant (i.e., $\pi_t$ is its equilibrium distribution). Given these choices, AIS proceeds by sampling $x_0 \sim \pi_0$, then for $t=1, \ldots, T$, sampling $x_t \sim F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right)$. Typically, $F_t$ might involve one or more MCMC steps targeting $\pi_t$. The final sample $x_T$ is then considered a draw from a proposal related to $\pi$.\label{ntn:transition_kernels}

To calculate importance weights for samples $x_T$, one would typically need the marginal proposal density $q_T(x_T)$ to compute $w_{\text{mar}}(x_T) = \hpi(x_T) / q_T(x_T)$. AIS considers an extended space over the entire path $x_{0:T} = \{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_T\}$.\label{ntn:extended_path_space} The proposal distribution over this extended path space is\label{ntn:ais_extended_proposal}
\begin{equation}
    Q\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\pi_0\left(x_0\right) \prod_{t=1}^T F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right). \label{eq:ais_extended_proposal}
\end{equation}
The marginal proposal density $q_t(x_t)$ at an intermediate step $t$ is then given by
\begin{equation}
    q_t\left(x_t\right)=\int q_{t-1}\left(x_{t-1}\right) F_t\left(x_t \mid x_{t-1}\right) \mathrm{d} x_{t-1} \text { for } t \geq 1, \quad q_0\left(x_0\right)=\pi_0\left(x_0\right). \label{eq:ais_marginal_proposal}
\end{equation}
However, computing $q_t(x_t)$ for $t \geq 1$ via this recursive integration is generally intractable, rendering the direct calculation of $w_{\text{mar}}(x_T)$ infeasible. AIS circumvents this issue by operating in the extended path space, defining a corresponding extended target distribution as follows:\label{ntn:ais_extended_target}
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\hat{\pi}\left(x_T\right) \prod_{t=1}^{T} B_{t+1}\left(x_t \mid x_{t+1}\right), \label{eq:ais_extended_target}
\end{equation}
where $B_{t+1}(x_t | x_{t+1})$ are backward Markov transition kernels, typically chosen such that $B_t$ leaves $\pi_t$ invariant when run in reverse (i.e., sampling $x_t$ from $x_{t+1}$). The importance weight for the entire path $x_{0:T}$ is then defined, similar to \Cref{eq:is_weights} as
\begin{equation}
    w\left(x_{0: T}\right)=\frac{\Gamma\left(x_{0: T}\right)}{Q\left(x_{0: T}\right)}. \label{eq:ais_weights}
\end{equation}
Crucially, these path weights can be computed efficiently and iteratively during the sampling process (see Algorithm~\ref{alg:ais}). The final samples $x_T^{(i)}$ are then weighted by $w(x_{0:T}^{(i)})$, allowing for the estimation of expectations with respect to $\pi_T$ using Equation~\eqref{eq:is_expectation_estimate} and the partition function with Equation~\eqref{eq:partition_function_estimate}.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Sequence of unnormalized distributions $\{{\pi}_t\}_{t=0}^T$ with ${\pi}_T := {\pi}$.
\Require Forward transition kernels $\{F_t\}_{t=1}^T$ with $F_t$ invariant to $\pi_t$.
\Require Backward transition kernels $\{B_{t+1}\}_{t=1}^T$ with $B_{t+1}$ invariant to $\pi_t$.
\State Sample $x_0^{(i)} \sim \pi_0(x)$ from initial distribution
\State Initialize weight $w^{(i)} = \pi_0(x_0^{(i)})$
\For{$t = 1$ to $T$}
    \State Sample $x_t^{(i)} \sim F_t(x_t \mid x_{t-1}^{(i)})$ from forward transition
    \State Update weight $w^{(i)} \leftarrow w^{(i)} \cdot \frac{F_t\left(x_t^{(i)} | x_{t-1}^{(i)}\right)}{B_{t}\left({x_{t-1}^{(i)} | x_{t}^{(i)}}\right)}$
\EndFor
\State Set $x^{(i)} = x_T^{(i)}$ as final sample
\State Update $w^{(i)} \leftarrow w^{(i)} \cdot \hpi(x^{(i)})$ \\
\Return $(x^{(i)}, w^{(i)})$
\end{algorithmic}
\caption{Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS)}
\label{alg:ais}
\end{algorithm}

\textcite{neal2001annealed} proposed using Metropolis-Hastings for $F_t$ and a specific backward kernel that satisfies the detailed balance property:
\begin{equation}
    B_{t+1}^{\text {Neal}}\left(x_t \mid x_{t+1}\right)=\frac{\pi_{t+1}\left(x_{t+1}\right) F_{t+1}\left(x_{t+1} \mid x_t\right)}{\pi_{t+1}\left(x_{t}\right)}. \label{eq:neal_backward_kernel}
\end{equation}
A closely related method is Unadjusted Langevin Annealing (ULA) \parencite{wu2020stochastic,welling2011bayesian,thin2021monte}. Here, the forward transition $F_{t+1}(x_{t+1}|x_t)$ (generating $x_{t+1}$ from $x_t$) uses Langevin dynamics targeting $\pi_{t+1}$ (instead of always targeting $\pi$ as in \Cref{eq:langevin_dynamics}):
\[
    x_{t+1} = x_t + \delta_t \nabla_x \ln \hpi_{t+1}(x_t) + \sqrt{2 \delta_t} \varepsilon_{t}, \quad \varepsilon_{t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
\]
Furthermore, the backwards kernel is defined quite simply as the reverse of the forward kernel, i.e. $B_t^{\text {ula }}(x_t \mid x_{t+1})=F_{t+1}(x_{t+1} \mid x_t)$.

AIS offers considerable flexibility in choosing the sequence of intermediate distributions $(\hpi_t)_{t=0}^T$, the forward transition kernels $(F_t)_{t=1}^T$, and the backward transition kernels $(B_t)_{t=0}^{T-1}$. The forward kernels $F_t$ must leave $\pi_t$ invariant; common choices include Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, or Langevin dynamics steps. The backward kernels $B_t$ can also be chosen strategically. Modern extensions of AIS even employ parametrised, learnable choices for $F_t$ and $B_t$, often trained to minimize the variance of the importance weights or facilitate end-to-end learning. The learnable forward kernels $F_t$ collectively define a transformation from the initial simple distribution $\pi_0$ to an approximation of the target $\pi_T$, while the backward kernels $B_t$ can be seen as attempting to reverse this transformation, conceptually aligning AIS with frameworks that learn explicit forward $p_\theta$ and reverse $q_\phi$ mappings between distributions, thereby connecting AIS to the broader variational perspectives discussed later (e.g., \Cref{eq:framework_mc}).

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of AIS}
    The AIS framework essentially involves a forward Markov chain, defined by kernels $(F_t)$, transforming an initial distribution $\mu$ towards a target $\pi$, and an auxiliary backward Markov chain, defined by $(B_t)$. In the continuous-time limit (as $T \rightarrow \infty$ and step sizes shrink appropriately), these discrete chains often converge to forward and backward stochastic differential equations (SDEs) (which we will make more concrete in \Cref{chapter:cmcd}). Furthermore, the extended proposal and target distributions converge to probability distributions over the continuous-time paths $\{x_t\}_{0 \leq t \leq T}$, which are termed \emph{path measures}, and denoted as follows:
    \[
        Q(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \rightP_{\text{fwd}}, \quad \Gamma(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \leftP_{\text{bwd}}, \label{eq:ais_path_measures}
    \]
    where the arrows signify the direction in time of the evolution of the corresponding SDE. Similarly, the importance sampling weights in \Cref{eq:ais_weights} converge to the density ratio between the forwards and backwards path measures in \Cref{eq:ais_path_measures}, which in turn is defined as a quantity called the Radon-Nikodym derivative:
    \[
        w(x_{0:T}) \rightarrow \frac{\rightP_{\text{fwd}}(x_{0:T})}{\leftP_{\text{bwd}}(x_{0:T})} \rightarrow \frac{\dd \rightP_{\text{fwd}}}{\dd \leftP_{\text{bwd}}}.
    \]
    We will see in \Cref{chapter:sde} that the Radon-Nikodym derivative is not well-defined for the density ratio of a forward path measure w.r.t a backward path measure, and will therefore motivate us to develop a novel theorem defining this quantity in \Cref{chapter:cmcd}.
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Generative Modelling}
\label{sec:gen_modelling}

The task of \emph{generative modelling} addresses scenarios where we lack an explicit form for the target distribution $\pi(x)$ or its gradients. Instead, we have access to a dataset of samples $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}} = \{x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{train}}}$ drawn from $\pi(x)$. The objective is to learn a parametric model $p_\theta(x)$ that approximates the underlying data distribution $\pi(x)$. This learned model serves two main purposes: generating new, novel samples that resemble those in $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$, and, in some cases, allowing for the evaluation of the approximated density $p_\theta(x)$ for given inputs. This paradigm has achieved remarkable success in the deep learning era, powering applications from image and language synthesis \citep{vaswani2017attention, song2021denoising} to breakthroughs in scientific domains like protein structure prediction and drug design \citep{jumper2021highly, watson2023novo, lin2023generating}.

Many generative modelling approaches frame the problem as learning a mapping between the complex data distribution $\pi(x)$ and a simpler, easy-to-sample latent distribution $\mu(z)$ (e.g., a standard Gaussian). One common strategy involves defining a generative process $p_\theta(x|z)$ that maps latent samples $z \sim \mu(z)$ to data space, and an inference process $q_\phi(z|x)$ that maps data $x$ to the latent space. The goal is to train these components such that sampling $z \sim \mu(z)$ and then $x \sim p_\theta(x|z)$ yields samples resembling the training data. The specific architectures and learning objectives for $p_\theta(x|z)$ and $q_\phi(z|x)$ define a wide range of algorithms. We begin by discussing Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs) \parencite{kingma2019introduction}, which learn the inference process as a variational approximation. We then turn our attention to a large collection of algorithms that focus on designing more flexible mappings using hierarchical latent variables (whether implicitly or explicitly), such as Normalising Flows (NFs) \parencite{papamakarios2021normalizing}, Score Matching (SM) \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score} and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) \parencite{ho2020denoising}. \label{ntn:discrete_mappings}

\subsection{Variational Auto-Encoders}

\nomenclature[z-vae]{$VAE$}{Variational Auto-Encoder}

VAEs parametrise $p_{\theta}(x|z)$ from the latent space $z$ to the data $x$ with a neural network called the \emph{decoder}, allowing us to map individual datapoints from $x^{(i)} \sim \Dtrain$ to corresponding latent variables $z^{(i)}$. Unfortunately, the reverse distribution $p(z \mid x)$ is intractable due to the integral involved in the marginal likelihood term $p_{\theta}(x)$, as can be seen from Bayes' rule
\begin{equation}
    p(z \mid x) = \frac{p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z)}{p_{\theta}(x)}, \quad p_{\theta}(x) = \int p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z) \dd z.
\end{equation}
Instead, a VAE learns a corresponding \textit{encoder} model $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ to approximate $p(z \mid x)$, by minimising the KL divergence between them. We can decompose this loss function as follows:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\DKL(q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \mid p(z \mid x)) &= \int q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \ln (\frac{p_{\theta}(x \mid z) \mu(z)}{q_{\phi}(z \mid x)}) \d q_{\phi} - \ln p_{\theta}(x) \int q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \d q_{\phi} \label{eq:vae_loss} \\
&= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[ \ln p_{\theta}(x \mid z) + \ln \mu(z) - \ln q_{\phi}(z \mid x) \right]}_{:= \text{ELBO}} - \ln p_{\theta}(x).  \label{eq:vae_loss_2}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Since the KL divergence is a non-negative quantity, we can equivalently maximise the ELBO defined in \Cref{eq:vae_loss_2} over the parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$. In general, we can see how the divergence-based loss in \Cref{eq:vae_loss} is alluring; we can simply parametrise mappings $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ and $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$, and learn the parameters by minimising the divergence between them. Historically, VAEs have struggled to learn sufficiently flexible mappings, resulting in poor-quality samples. In the next sections, we improve upon the flexibility of our mappings by allowing a more hierarchical structure.

\subsection{Normalising Flows}

Normalising Flows (NFs) offer an alternative approach by constructing a bijective (invertible and differentiable) mapping $f_\theta$ between the latent space to the data space. If $z \sim \mu(z)$ is a sample from the base distribution, then $x = f_\theta(z)$ is a sample from the target distribution $p_\theta(x)$. The inverse mapping $z = f_\theta^{-1}(x)$ must also be efficiently computable. Training typically involves maximizing $\log p_\theta(x)$ for $x \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$. NFs achieve expressive power by composing a sequence of simpler invertible transformations $f_\theta = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1$. While powerful, the requirement for invertible transformations with tractable Jacobian determinants imposes architectural constraints, though significant progress has been made in designing flexible flow layers (e.g., coupling layers, autoregressive flows). However, we begin to see how the concept of iterative, learnable transformations is central to many advanced generative models, including those explored later in this thesis.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Normalising Flows and SDEs}
    The idea of composing many simple transformations in Normalising Flows has a natural continuous-time limit. If we consider an infinitesimal transformation parametrised by time $t$, $x_{t+\delta t} \approx x_t + v(x_t, t)\delta t$, this can be described by an ordinary differential equation (ODE): $\frac{dx}{dt} = v(x_t, t)$, where $x_0 \sim \mu$ and $x_T$ follows the target distribution $p_\theta(x)$. Such models are known as Continuous Normalising Flows (CNFs) or FFJORD \parencite{grathwohl2018ffjord}. Recently, \emph{Flow Matching} techniques \parencite{lipman2022flow} have emerged, which also learn such continuous transformations by directly regressing a time-dependent vector field $v(x, t)$ that satisfies the continuity equation or transports probability mass between distributions. Crucially, these deterministic ODE-based transformations defined by $v(x,t)$ often have corresponding stochastic differential equation (SDE) formulations, e.g., by adding a noise term: $\d x_t = v(x_t,t)\d t + g(x_t,t)\d \text{``noise''}$. This SDE perspective directly connects NFs, CNFs, and Flow Matching to the framework of score-based generative models and diffusion models (discussed next), which also leverage SDEs to define transformations between noise and data distributions. This continuous-time view, encompassing both ODEs and SDEs, will be instrumental in the unifying frameworks developed in this thesis.
\end{lightgraybox}

\subsection{Score Matching}
\label{sec:sm}

Energy-based approaches (such as EBMs) learn a parametrised representation of $\hat{\pi}$, and run into the issue of having to estimate the intractable normalising constant. On the other hand, Score Matching (SM) \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score} attempts to learn a different quantity, the \emph{score} of the probability distribution. This quantity is defined as the gradient of the log-probability distribution, which gets rid of the necessity of learning the normalising constant,\label{ntn:score}\label{ntn:score_net_discrete}
\begin{equation}
    s_{\theta}(x) \approx \nabla_x \ln \hat{\pi}(x) = - \nabla_x f_{\theta}(x) - \underbrace{\nabla_x Z_{\theta}}_{0}.
\end{equation}
Vanilla score-matching models are usually learnt by minimising the Fisher divergence between the parametrised score function and the actual quantity of interest, giving us 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{VSM}} = \mathbb{E}_{\hat{\pi}} \left[ \| \nabla_x \ln \hat{\pi} (x) - s_{\theta}(x) \|_2^2 \right].
\end{equation}
Due to the structure of the loss function taking an expectation over samples from $\hat{\pi}$, vanilla score-matching techniques often really struggle in approximating $\hat{\pi}$ in regions of low probability where samples are sparse. In order to address this issue, the authors resort to a technique that we will see repeated quite often in the current landscape: \textit{iterative noising}. Instead of learning a score model for $\hat{\pi}$ directly, score-matching creates noisier and noisier versions of $\hat{\pi}$ by adding successively larger amounts of Gaussian noise, and learns a score model per level of noise. If we assume a schedule of noise values $\sigma_1 \leq \ldots \leq \sigma_T$, we can write down each noised distribution as 
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{t}(x) = \int \hat{\pi} (w) \mathcal{N}(x; w, \sigma_t^2) \dd w, \quad t = 1, \ldots, T. \label{eq:sm_noised_distributions}
\end{equation}
We can then modify our score model to learn a different parametrisation for a different noise scale, resulting in a Noise-Conditional Score Network (NCSN) given by $s_{\theta}(x, t)$ and a weighted sum of Fisher divergences per noise scale, given as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SM}} = \sum_{t=1}^T \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t}} \left[ \| \nabla_x \ln \pi_{t} (x) - s_{\theta}(x, t) \|_2^2 \right]. \label{eq:sm_loss}
\end{equation}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of Score-Matching}
    As shown in \textcite{song2020score}, the iterative noising process can be represented as a stochastic differential equation in the continuous limit. Here, we redefine our notation to follow standard notation in the literature closely, and assume that the distribution at $t=0$ corresponds to the data distribution $\pi$, and the distribution at $t=T$ corresponds to the base distribution $\mu$. In this case, the iterative noising process can be represented as a forward SDE, transporting samples from $\pi$ to $\mu$, and is given by 
    \[
        \dd X_t = \sqrt{\frac{\dd \sigma_t^2}{\dd t}} \dd W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \pi, \quad t = 0, \ldots, T, \label{eq:sm_sde_fwd}
    \]
    where $W_t$ is a quantity that refers to the derivative of stochastic noise termed the Wiener process (which we will define rigorously in \Cref{chapter:sde}). The joint distribution of all intermediate random variables in the path of this SDE is represented by a quantity termed the \emph{path measure}, i.e. $\mathbb{P}(X_0, \ldots, X_T) := \rightP$. As we will see in \Cref{chapter:sde}, it is possible to define a ``reversal'' of an SDE that shares the same marginal probability distributions as the original SDE, but travels in the opposite direction in time, transporting the base distribution $\mu$ to the data distribution $\pi$. We will show later that the only difference in the backward SDE is the addition of an extra term in the drift equal to $\nabla_{X_t} \ln p(X_t)$, which is exactly the score function we were attempting to learn! Concretely, we approximate this term with a score model, which gives us the backward SDE
    \[
        \dd X_t &= - C \underbrace{\nabla_{X_t} \ln p(X_t)}_{\approx s_{\theta}(X_t, t)}  \dd t +  \sqrt{\frac{\dd \sigma_t^2}{\dd t}} \dd \overleftarrow{W}_t, \quad X_T 
        \sim \mu \label{eq:heuristic_nelsons_reverse}
    \]
    for a Wiener process $\overleftarrow{W}_t$ defined in the reverse time direction, some variable $C$, and a corresponding path measure $\leftP_{\theta}$. As we will show more concretely in \Cref{chapter:cmcd}, the loss function for score-matching as defined in \Cref{eq:sm_loss} converges to minimising the KL divergence between the path measures $\rightP$ and $\leftP_{\theta}$. Therefore, we need a way to define the KL divergence between path measures of SDEs travelling in opposite directions of time, needing a novel theorem for the density ratio between these path measures.
\end{lightgraybox}

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models}
\label{sec:ddpm}

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) \parencite{ho2020denoising} are another popular framework for generative modelling using the notion of ``iterative noising'' that have found massive success. DDPM imposes additional structure on the mapping from data to latent $q_{\phi}(z \mid x)$ by introducing a series of additional latent variables\footnote{We note here that this convention is the opposite of that considered by sampling techniques such as AIS, but consistent with the notation chosen by Score Matching algorithms, as can be seen from \Cref{eq:sm_sde_fwd}.} $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}\}$ with $x_0 := x$ and $x_T := z$, and assuming a Markov assumption on the chain of latents, such that $x_t$ given $x_{t-1}$ is independent of previous latents. The authors then specifically choose a form of "noising" process that iteratively adds Gaussian noise to each latent at time $t-1$ to obtain the latent at time $t$, giving us the noising distributions
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t ; \sqrt{1 - \beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I), \label{eq:ddpm_noising}
\end{equation}
for some choice of noising $\beta_t$ schedule. \label{ntn:ddpm_noise}\label{ntn:ddpm_alpha}\label{ntn:ddpm_alpha_bar_ch2}Due to each individual noising process being Gaussian, a nice property of the DDPM formulation is that we can represent the latent at any time step $t$ w.r.t the original data sample $x = x_0$ in closed-form\footnote{See Appendix A for a proof.}, giving us
\begin{equation}
    q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\left(x_t ; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1 - \bar{\alpha}_t I)\right), \quad \bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{t=1}^T \alpha_t\text{ with } \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t.
\end{equation}
If we could now learn a reverse "denoising" process $p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ that takes a sample from the noising distribution at time $t$ and maps it back to the original data distribution iteratively, we could sample from the original data distribution by starting with a sample from the base distribution $x_T \sim \pi_0$ and iteratively applying the reverse noising process. However, calculating the exact reverse noising process is intractable, as we see here
\begin{align}
    p(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) &= \frac{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) p(x_{t-1})}{p(x_t)} \\
    &= \frac{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) \int q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0) \pi_0(x_0) \dd x_0}{\int q(x_t \mid x_0) \pi_0(x_0) \dd x_0},
\end{align}
where the integrals over the data distribution $\pi_0$ are intractable. Instead, DDPM assumes a variational distribution $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t-1}; \mu_{\theta}(x_t, t), \Sigma_{\theta}(x_t, t))$ that is a Gaussian with mean and variance parametrised by a neural network, and learns the parameters $\theta$ in the same way as a VAE (where the latents $z = \{x_1, \ldots, x_{T}\}$). We can therefore write down an ELBO similar to \Cref{eq:vae_loss}, giving us
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{DDPM}} = \mathbb{E}_{x_{1:T} \sim q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \left[ \ln \left[ \frac{p_{\theta}(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)}{q(x_{1:T} \mid x_0)} \right] \right].
\end{equation}
Interestingly, \textcite{song2020score} showed that in the continuous limit, the iterative noising process for DDPM converges to an SDE (different from the SDE in Score Matching), and therefore both Score Matching and DDPM are variants of a score-matching loss, albeit on different forward SDEs.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Continuous Limit of DDPM}
    As shown in \textcite{song2020score}, the iterative noising process in \Cref{eq:ddpm_noising} can be represented as a stochastic differential equation in the continuous limit. Making the same assumptions as Score Matching ($t=0$ corresponds to the data distribution $\hpi$ and $t=T$ corresponds to the base distribution $\mu$), the forward SDE is given by
    \[
        \dd X_t = -\frac{1}{2} \beta_t X_t \dd t + \sqrt{\beta_t} \dd W_t, \quad X_0 \sim \pi. \label{eq:ddpm_sde_fwd}
    \]
    The DDPM objective, which is equivalent to the Score Matching objective \parencite{song2020score}, can therefore be written in terms of the KL divergence between the forward and backward path measures.
\end{lightgraybox}


\section{Towards Unification: A hierarchical Variational Perspective}

\label{sec:unifying_framework_discrete}

The preceding sections have reviewed a diverse array of sampling and generative modelling techniques that follow a recurring theme, whether explicit or implicit: the concept of defining or learning a transformation (or \emph{mapping}) between a simple, tractable base distribution $\mu(z)$ (where $z$ can be considered a latent variable) and the more complex target data distribution $\pi(x)$. For instance, VAEs explicitly model a decoder $p_\theta(x|z)$ and an encoder $q_\phi(z|x)$. NFs learn an invertible mapping $x=f_\theta(z)$. DDPMs and SM models implicitly define a \emph{noising} process that transforms data $x$ to noise $z \sim \mu(z)$, and then learn to reverse this process, effectively learning $p_\theta(x|z)$ through iterative refinement. Even AIS, with its sequence of forward kernels $F_t$, can be viewed as constructing a path from $\mu(z)$ to $\pi(x)$, with backward kernels $B_t$ hinting at a reverse path. This common thread suggests the potential for a unifying perspective based on characterising and learning these transformations.

A natural way to learn the parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$ is by minimizing a divergence between the joint distribution induced by the 'data $\to$ latent' and vice-versa. One general form for such an objective, connecting the forward and reverse processes, can be expressed by minimizing a divergence $\mathcal{D}$ between a "forward" joint $p_\theta(x,z) = p_\theta(x|z)\mu(z)$ and a "reverse" joint $q_\phi(x,z) = q_\phi(z|x)\pi(x)$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:divergence_loss_conceptual}
    \mathcal{L}_D(\theta, \phi) = \mathcal{D}\left( q_\phi(z|x)\pi(x) \mid\mid p_\theta(x|z)\mu(z) \right).
\end{equation}
The central insight of the unifying framework we propose is that many iterative algorithms, particularly those with SDE limits like Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) algorithms \parencite{neal2001annealed,jarzynski1997equilibrium,jarzynski1997nonequilibrium}, Score Matching algorithms \parencite{song2019generative,song2020score}, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) \parencite{ho2020denoising}, can be understood as specific instantiations or approximations of minimizing such a divergence, especially when $p_\theta$ and $q_\phi$ are structured as \emph{hierarchical, multi-step latent variable models}.

Specifically, we adopt a hierarchical latent variable model where the transformation from $z$ (denoted $x_0$) to $x$ (denoted $x_T$) and vice-versa occurs through a sequence of intermediate states $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{T-1}\}$. The overall forward mapping $p_\theta(x_T \mid x_0)$ and reverse mapping $q_\phi(x_0 \mid x_T)$ are then factorised as products of simpler, conditional transition kernels:
\[
    p_{\theta}(x_T, \ldots, x_1 \mid x_0) = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} p_{\theta_{t}}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t}), \quad q_{\phi}(x_0, \ldots, x_{T-1} \mid x_T) = \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} q_{\phi_{t}}(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}).
\]
While we can choose arbitrarily complex functions to define the intermediate mappings $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$\label{ntn:discrete_mappings_intermediate}, as $T \to \infty$ and we consider the continuous limit, even a simple choice for $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$ such as Gaussian distributions can yield highly flexible mappings. This allows us to write down the hierarchical mappings in the form of Markov chains where $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$ represent the forward and backward transition kernels:
\begin{align}
    x_{t+1} \leftarrow x_{t} + f_{\theta_t}(x_t) + \sigma_t \varepsilon_{t+1}, \quad p_{\theta_t}(x_{t+1} \mid x_{t}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t+1}; x_{t} + \frac{f_{\theta_t}(x_t)}{T}, \sigma_t^2 I), \\
    x_{t} \leftarrow x_{t+1} + b_{\phi_t}(x_{t+1}) + \sigma_t \varepsilon_{t}, \quad q_{\phi_t}(x_{t} \mid x_{t+1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_{t}; x_{t+1} + \frac{b_{\phi_t}(x_{t+1})}{T}, \sigma_t^2 I), \label{eq:framework_mc}
\end{align}
where $\{\varepsilon_t\}_{t=1}^{T}$ are i.i.d. standard normal random variables, and $f_{\theta_t}$ and $b_{\phi_t}$ are parametrised functions (such as neural networks)\label{ntn:param_fns}. In this setup, there are potentially infinite solutions that satisfy the divergence loss, so we must make further choices and assumptions about either the forward mapping or the reverse mapping (or both).
%  down the divergence over the extended space, and using the data-processing inequality \parencite{cover1999elements}, we can show that the divergence between the extended mappings is an upper bound over \Cref{eq:divergence_loss_conceptual}:

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: The Continuous Limit}
    \label{sec:continuous_limit}
    In the limit of infinite steps, where $T \to \infty$, the transition densities governing the mappings between latent variables $t$ and $t+1$ can be written down as the solutions to a pair of stochastic differential equations (SDEs)\footnote{This remark is a brief introduction to the continuous limit of the mappings $p_{\theta_t}$ and $q_{\phi_t}$. We introduce the notation and terminology more rigorously in \Cref{chapter:sde}.} given by
    \[
        \mathrm{d}X_t = f_{\theta_t}(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_t \mathrm{d}W_t, \quad \mathrm{d}X_t = b_{\phi_t}(X_t) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma_t \mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{W}_t, \label{eq:fwd_bwd_sde_sketch}
    \]
    where $\mathrm{d}W_t$ is the ``forward'' Wiener process, and $\mathrm{d} \overleftarrow{W}_t$ is the ``backward'' Wiener process. In this continuous limit, the joint distribution of the latent variables further converge to a quantity termed the ``path'' measure of the corresponding SDEs, which we refer to as $\rightP_{\theta}$ and $\leftP_{\phi}$ respectively. If we are able to define a valid probability divergence measure between the forward and backwards path measure, we can rewrite \Cref{eq:divergence_loss_conceptual} in the continuous limit as
    \[
        \mathcal{L}_D(\theta, \phi) = \mathcal{L}_D(\rightP_{\theta} \| \leftP_{\phi}).
    \]
    As we will motivate in \Cref{chapter:sde}, it is non-trivial to easily define a valid probability divergence measure between the forward and backwards path measure, and as one of our main contributions, we will develop a unique change of measure between forward and backwards path measures that will enable us to use this framework to represent a large family of existing algorithms.
\end{lightgraybox}

\section{Conditional Sampling}

Having explored methods for unconditional sampling and generative modelling, and having noted the emerging role of stochastic processes and variational viewpoints, we now turn to another critical paradigm: conditional sampling. As briefly introduced at the beginning of this chapter, the goal here is to draw samples $x$ from a distribution $\pi(x \mid y)$ that is dependent on some observed condition, context, or measurement $y$. This problem manifests in numerous ways, from generating images based on text prompts to solving inverse problems in scientific imaging. We broadly categorize approaches into conditional generative modelling, where a model for $\pi(x \mid y)$ is learned, and pathwise conditioning, where samples from an unconditional distribution are directly transformed.

\subsection{Conditional Generative Modeling}

In \emph{conditional generative modelling}, the primary objective is to learn a parametric model $p_{\theta, \text{cond}}(x \mid y)$ that approximates the true conditional distribution $\pi(x \mid y)$. This often leverages an existing unconditional distribution $\pi(x)$ (or learned approximation $p_\theta(x)$). The conditional distribution $\pi(x \mid y)$ can often be expressed through a ``tilting'' of the unconditional base distribution $\pi(x)$ either via a general reward \label{ntn:reward_function}or energy function $r(x, y)$, leading to $\pi(x \mid y) \propto \pi(x) \exp (r(x, y))$, or more explicitly through a likelihood $p(y \mid x)$ as in Bayes' theorem.\label{ntn:likelihood} Learning $p_\theta(x \mid y)$ typically requires a dataset of paired observations $\mathcal{D}_{\text {cond }}=\left\{\left(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text {cond }}}$, often with the (somewhat strong) assumption that we have a-priori access to a mapping to the unconditional distribution.

% In a generative modelling setting, we have access to the unconditional distribution $\hpi$ through the mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$, and we wish to instead learn a mapping to a modified distribution $\piy$ that is a "tilted" or "conditioned" version of $\hpi$ based on some additional constraints. We can write this additional constraint as either a generic reward function $r(x)$ that results in $\piy \propto \hpi \exp(r(x))$, or an explicit likelihood model $p(y \mid x)$ that results in $\piy \propto \hpi p(y \mid x)$. In this situation, we attempt to learn a conditional mapping $p_{\cdot \mid y}(x \mid y, z)$ that modifies the original mapping $p_{\theta}(x \mid z)$ based on a dataset of datapoints and conditions $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cond}} = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{cond}}}$. This approach is termed \textit{conditional generative modelling}, as we eventually learn a way to map from a latent space to the conditioned distribution, often with the (somewhat strong) assumption that we have a-priori access to a map to the unconditional distribution. 
A particularly active area for conditional generative modelling is within the framework of score-based generative models and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs). Recall that these models learn the score of progressively noised versions of the data, $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x)$. For conditional generation, the aim shifts to learning or approximating the score of the \emph{conditional} noised distribution, $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x|y)$. Using Bayes' rule on the noised distributions, this conditional score can be decomposed:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_x \log \pi_t(x|y) = \nabla_x \log \pi_t(x) + \nabla_x \log p(y|x_t), \label{eq:conditional_score_bayes}
\end{equation}
where $p(y|x_t)$ is the time-dependent likelihood of observing $y$ given the noised state $x_t$. This term $\nabla_x \log p(y|x_t)$ is often referred to as ``guidance'',\label{ntn:guidance_ch2} as it steers the reverse mapping (which normally uses $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x)$) towards samples consistent with $y$. However, $p(y|x_t) = \int p(y|x_0) p(x_0|x_t) \d x_0$ is generally intractable. Various techniques have been proposed to approximate this guidance term or to learn the conditional score $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x|y)$ more directly, which we discuss briefly below.

\paragraph{Classifier Guidance.} Classifier Guidance methods \parencite{dhariwal2021diffusion} train an additional classifier $p_\phi(y|x_t)$ on noisy data $x_t$ and uses its gradient $\nabla_{x_t} \log p_\phi(y|x_t)$ as an approximation to the true guidance term. This allows leveraging powerful pre-trained unconditional diffusion models, but requires the training of expensive classifier models that need to learn to classify $y$ from progressively noised versions of $x_0$.

\paragraph{Reconstruction Guidance.} Reconstruction Guidance methods, such as Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) \parencite{chung2022diffusion} and $\Pi$GDM \parencite{song2022pseudoinverse}, typically approximate the intractable $p(y|x_t)$ through isotropic or low-rank Gaussian distributions. These methods require knowledge of the explicit form of the likelihood $p(y \mid x)$, and are particularly suited for inverse problems where $y = \mathcal{A}(x_0) + \text{noise}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ is a known forward operator.

\paragraph{Classifier-Free Guidance.} To avoid training a separate classifier or make approximations to the conditional likelihood, Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) \parencite{ho2022classifier} learns a single score network $s_\theta(x_t, t, y)$ that is trained to predict the noise (or score) for both conditional and unconditional generation. This is achieved by randomly dropping the conditioning information $y$ (e.g., replacing it with a null token $\emptyset$) during training with a certain probability. The model thus learns to approximate $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x|y)$ when $y$ is provided, and $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x)$ when $y$ is dropped. At inference time, the term $\nabla_x \pi_t(x \mid y)$ is approximated as a weighted sum of these two terms.
\paragraph{Amortised Conditional Training.} Instead of relying on Bayes' rule (Eq. \ref{eq:conditional_score_bayes}) and approximating the guidance term, another line of work aims to directly learn the conditional score $\nabla_x \log \pi_t(x|y)$ from paired data $(x_0, y)$. Methods like Conditional Denoising Estimators (CDE) \parencite{batzolis2021conditional} train a network $s_\theta(x_t, t, y)$ by minimizing a conditional score matching objective, e.g., $\mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, y, x_t \sim p(x_t|x_0,y)} [\| s_\theta(x_t, t, y) - \nabla_{x_t} \log p(x_t|x_0,y) \|^2]$. This approach requires access to paired data for every new conditioning task but can be very effective if such data is available.
\paragraph{Replacement Sampling.} For specific conditioning types, particularly inpainting or where $y$ represents a known subset of $x_0$'s coordinates (i.e., $y = x_0^{[M]}$), Replacement Sampling is a simple heuristic approach. During the reverse diffusion process that generates $x_t$ from $x_{t+dt}$, the coordinates of $x_t$ corresponding to the known parts $y$ are replaced with their ground truth values from $y$. This effectively forces parts of the sample to remain consistent with the observation. While heuristic, it has been used effectively in applications like protein motif scaffolding \parencite{watson2023novo}.

\subsection{Towards Unification: A Conditional SDE Perspective}

The diversity of the approaches we have discussed, especially within the diffusion model paradigm, highlights both the flexibility and the somewhat ad-hoc nature of current conditional generation techniques. Many rely on approximations or specific training schemes. Many of these algorithms have been historically motivated heuristically, without any theoretical guarantees on the obtained samples belonging to the correct conditional distribution. Furthermore, algorithms such as Classifier Guidance were developed in the discrete diffusion model framework, making it difficult to see how they relate to diffusion models in the continuous limit. This motivates the search for a more fundamental, unifying theory.

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: The Continuous Limit for Conditional Generative Modelling}
    The common thread in many conditional generation methods is the modification of the reverse mapping from the latent space $z$ to the data $x$. In the continuous limit, the underlying generative modelling approaches converge to SDEs, as we discussed previously (\Cref{eq:heuristic_nelsons_reverse}). For conditional generation, the drift term in the reverse process is altered, including an additional \emph{conditional} score term, giving us:
    \[
        \dd X_t &= - C_1 \underbrace{\nabla_{X_t} \ln p(X_t)}_{\approx s_{\theta}(X_t, t, y)}  - C_2 \nabla_{X_t} \ln p(y \mid X_t) \dd t +  \sqrt{\frac{\dd \sigma_t^2}{\dd t}} \dd \overleftarrow{W}_t.
    \]
    The term $\nabla_{X_t} \ln p(y \mid X_t)$ is the challenging guidance. As we will rigorously establish in \Cref{chapter:deft}, Doob's $h$-transform from SDE theory provides a principled way to derive the exact modification to the drift of an SDE when conditioned on an event or an observation $y$. This transform introduces a specific correction term, $h(X_t, t, y)$, related to the probability of the observation given the process's state. Many of the heuristic methods discussed above (Reconstruction Guidance, Classifier Guidance, and even interpretations of CFG and amortised training) can be understood as different ways of approximating or learning this $h$-transform or its effects on the score. This Doob's $h$-transform perspective will form the basis of our unifying framework for conditional sampling in \Cref{chapter:deft}, allowing us to analyze existing methods and propose new, efficient algorithms like DEFT with stronger theoretical underpinnings.
\end{lightgraybox}

\subsection{Pathwise Conditioning}

While conditional generative modelling, as discussed previously, typically focuses on learning a parametric approximation $p_{\theta, \text{cond}}(x|y)$ to the entire conditional distribution, an alternative paradigm, \emph{pathwise conditioning}, sidesteps this distributional perspective. Instead, it directly addresses the question: \emph{can we define a procedure to transform individual samples drawn from an unconditional distribution $\pi(x)$ so that they effectively become samples from a desired conditional distribution $\pi(x|y)$?} This approach focuses on deriving a ``correction factor'' or transformation applicable at the sample level, rather than re-parameterising a global density model. This is particularly useful when it may be computationally or mathematically intractable to learn an entire mapping from a simple latent distribution to the unconditional (or conditional) distribution, but it is still possible to obtain samples quite efficiently.

% Here, we digress somewhat from our discussions so far on unnormalised density estimation and generative modelling, and consider a different problem: can we ``transform'' individual samples from an unconditional distribution to instead have been generated from a conditional distribution? To rephrase this problem, we can equivalently ask if we can calculate the ``correction factor'' that when added to a sample from the unconditional distribution, results in a sample from the conditional distribution. This problem is called \textit{pathwise conditioning}, and is the focus of this section.

Pathwise conditioning is applicable when the unconditional distribution $\pi(x)$ and the likelihood $p(y|x)$ belong to a family that permits the analytical derivation of such a sample-wise correction. \emph{Gaussian distributions} stand out as a family of distributions where this is feasible. While learning a full parametric mapping (as in conditional generative modelling) might be complex or data-intensive, if the underlying structure is Gaussian, direct sample transformation can be highly efficient. In this thesis, our exploration of pathwise conditioning will primarily focus on its application within Bayesian inference, specifically for models built upon Gaussian Processes (GPs) \parencite{williams2006gaussian}. This focus is motivated by our aim to develop scalable, sampling-based Bayesian methods where posterior samples, rather than the full posterior distribution, are used for inference and model selection.

% This situation can arise in specific scenarios. For example, the unconditonal distribution and likelihood might belong to a specific family of distributions that allow us to calculate the ``correction factor'' in closed-form. In other cases, it may be computationally or mathematically intractable to learn an entire mapping from a simple latent distribution to the unconditional (or conditional) distribution, but it is still possible to obtain samples quite efficiently. In this thesis, we focus on applying the framework of pathwise conditioning to the task of Bayesian inference. The reasons for this are two-fold: first, we will develop a sampling-based framework for Bayesian inference that allows us to obtain uncertainty estimates and perform model selection using a finite number of samples from the posterior distribution, and second, we will show how a large family of Bayesian inference algorithms can be approximated by a family of algorithms that allows us to perform pathwise conditioning, i.e., Gaussian Processes \parencite{williams2006gaussian}.

% As the name suggests, Gaussian distributions are a very useful family of distributions that allow us to calculate the ``correction factor'' in closed-form using a simple formula known as Matheron's rule \parencite{journel1976mining, chiles2012geostatistics, wilson2021pathwise}. In simple terms, Matheron's rule provides us a way to calculate the ``correction factor'' to update a Gaussian random variable conditioned on observations of another Gaussian random variable.

Indeed, for jointly Gaussian random variables, Matheron's rule \parencite{journel1976mining, chiles2012geostatistics, wilson2021pathwise} provides an explicit formula for this correction factor, enabling the transformation of samples from a joint (or prior) Gaussian distribution into samples from a conditional (or posterior) Gaussian distribution.

\begin{theorem}[Matheron's rule]
    \label{thm:matherons_rule}
Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be jointly Gaussian, centered\footnote{zero-mean.} random variables. Then, the random variable $X_1$, conditioned on an observation $X_2 = x$ is expressed as
\[
    X_1 \mid X_2 = x \stackrel{\mathrm{d}}{=} X_1 + \Sigma_{X_1, X_2} \Sigma_{X_2, X_2}^{-1} (x - X_2).
\]
An individual sample $(\hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2)$ from the joint distribution $\mathbb{P}(X_1, X_2)$ can be transformed to a sample from the conditional distribution $\mathbb{P}(X_1 \mid X_2 = x)$ by applying the Matheron transformation, given by
\[
    \hat{x}_1 \leftarrow \hat{x}_1 + \Sigma_{X_1, X_2} \Sigma_{X_2, X_2}^{-1} (x - \hat{x}_2).
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
See \textcite[Theorem 1, Pg. 4]{wilson2021pathwise}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lightgraybox}{Remark: Infinite-Dimensional Limit of Gaussian Distributions}
    Matheron's rule, as stated in \Cref{thm:matherons_rule}, applies to finite-dimensional jointly Gaussian random variables. However, its power extends significantly when we consider the infinite-dimensional limit in \emph{Bayesian Nonparametrics}. Here, instead of a finite set of parameters, we might place a prior over an entire function, and we consider potentially \emph{infinite} sets of random variables representing such functions. In this setting, finite-dimensional probability distributions converge to stochastic processes, which we've already seen in the context of being solutions to SDEs. Specifically, we consider Gaussian Processes (GPs), a family of stochastic processes representing functions $f$ where any finite collection of function values $\{f(x_i)\}$ is jointly Gaussian. As we will detail in \Cref{chapter:gp_sampling} (see \Cref{thm:matherons_rule_gp}), a sample $f(\cdot)$ drawn from a GP prior can be transformed into a sample path from the GP posterior (conditioned on data $y$) using a direct analogue of the finite-dimensional correction. This ``pathwise'' view, where entire sample functions from the prior are modified to yield posterior sample functions, is central to developing scalable sampling-based inference for GPs. This perspective moves beyond discrete latent states $X_t$ indexed by "time" $t$ (as in diffusion models) to continuous stochastic processes $f(x)$ indexed by coordinates $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
\end{lightgraybox}
