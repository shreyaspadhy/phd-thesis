Machine learning has revolutionised the way we approach modelling and decision making in numerous fields-ranging from healthcare to scientific discovery, defining a new paradigm to how we analyse data and derive insights from complex systems. While the entire gamut of these applications and methodologies are vast and varied, there is often a \emph{crucial} common thread: the need to obtain samples from complex, high-dimensional probability distributions. This has allowed us to develop powerful applications, such as image synthesis, protein structure prediction, and drug design, to name a few \citep{jumper2021highly, larson2019evaluation, gupta2021deep, roy2022does}. 

A large family of methods focus on \textit{generative modelling}: the task of estimating a probability distribution from observed samples to generate new ones. The approaches have evolved from one-shot samplers such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \citep{goodfellow2014generative} to iterative samplers like diffusion models \citep{ho2020denoising, song2020score}, dramatically improving the quality of samples, often at the cost of speed. Another family of ML algorithms centres on \textit{obtaining samples from unnormalised probability distributions}, where we can only evaluate the density of a given sample. Here, classical Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques remain indispensable, though recent innovations in neural sampling algorithms promise to boost efficiency and coverage of the target distribution \citep{doucet2022score, zhang2022fast, vargas2023denoising}.


High-quality samples are therefore crucial for unlocking impactful downstream applications. Many modern \textit{Bayesian inference} algorithms, for instance, rely on approximating quantities of interest using high-quality samples from the posterior distribution. Within \textit{Bayesian Deep Learning} this translates into obtaining posterior samples for Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs)--flexible deep learning models capable of expressing the uncertainty of their predictions. While classical MCMC methods such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) \citep{duane1987hybrid} and the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) \citep{hoffman2014no} can, in principle, supply these samples, their cost scales poorly with network size. Recent approximate techniques, notably the \emph{Linearised Laplace} method \citep{mackay1992bayesian, immer2021improving, antoran2022adapting}, have begun to connect BNN methodology to \textit{Bayesian nonparametrics}. Here, flexible models like Gaussian Processes (GPs) shift the computational burden from parameter count to observation count. In both situations, the sampling problem focuses on drawing samples from a high-dimensional--albeit \emph{structured}--Gaussian, which can still scale cubically (either in the parameters or observations).

Sampling is no less central in other subfields. \textit{Reinforcement Learning (RL)}, for example, inherently involves sampling trajectories to estimate values, learn policies, and guide exploration of vast state-action spaces, with strategies like importance sampling for off-policy learning and MCMC-inspired exploration becoming crucial \citep{zhou2020posterior, tiapkin2022optimistic, katdare2023marginalized}. Even in the era of \textit{Large Language Models (LLMs)}, where text is generated token by token, \textit{how we sample} from the model's predicted distribution fundamentally dictates output quality. Techniques like nucleus and temperature sampling are essential, carefully balancing creativity with coherence to avoid the repetitive or nonsensical outputs often produced by simpler decoding schemes like greedy search \citep{holtzman2019curious, du2025optimizing}.

\paragraph{Challenges in sampling algorithms and the way forward.}{

Across these domains and applications, we see common challenges. Firstly, high dimensionality poses a significant hurdle; for instance, posterior distributions for uncertainty estimation in neural networks can span millions of dimensions, complicating the acquisition of high-quality samples. Secondly, there is always a trade-off between exactness and efficiency; choices can range from exact MCMC algorithms to efficient-but-approximate Variational Inference (VI) algorithms. Algorithms that can improve along these two axes have immense value in both allowing us to scale these techniques over larger and more complex data, as well as improve the accuracy of existing methods.

While these fields have often been explored independently in the literature, there has historically been cross-pollination: ideas from statistical physics such as annealing and tempering now form the basis of the best sampling techniques; probabilistic inference tools such as HMC and VI have been adapted and scaled for deep learning; and advancements in deep learning models, in turn, fuel better neural samplers and generative models. As models and data grow in size, and our ambition to apply machine learning to increasingly complex domains grows, efficient and accurate sampling techniques will remain a crucial area of research.
}

\section{The Goal of this Thesis}

This thesis aims to advance the field of sampling by developing and analysing a versatile family of algorithms. Depending on the specific task, these algorithms draw from variational inference, stochastic optimisation, or stochastic control theory. Our overarching contributions address two critical needs: firstly, the establishment of unifying theoretical frameworks--leveraging the mathematics of stochastic processes to model transformations between distributions--to understand the rapidly evolving landscape of generative modelling and sampling; and secondly, the development of novel stochastic optimisation techniques to drastically scale Bayesian inference--where stochastic processes reappear, this time as flexible priors. These objectives are pursued across the following three core areas of research.

\subsection{A Unifying Framework for Generative Modelling and Sampling}

Recent years have seen an increased interest in iterative noising algorithms for generative modelling and sampling, approached from diverse viewpoints. Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) design Markov chains that gradually add noise to data, learning to reverse this process via variational inference. Conversely, Score Matching (SM) techniques directly learn data distribution gradients through regression. Concurrently, there has been a resurgence in techniques that learn \emph{mappings} between distributions, such as flow matching and optimal transport. Sampling literature has also shown a keen interest in extending Annealed Importance Sampling (AIS) methods, drawing inspiration from diffusion models and optimal transport. Many of these algorithms conceptualise the transformation between different distributions through the lens of a continuous-time stochastic process.

With such an explosion of techniques-rooted variously in variational inference, optimal transport, and stochastic control-a truly unifying perspective has, until now, been notably absent. While some work links many of these algorithms to Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs), a comprehensive framework encompassing both generative modelling and sampling remains elusive. This thesis addresses this gap, by introducing a hierarchical variational-inference perspective. Crucially, we develop a novel method for computing divergences \emph{pathwise}\footnote{We will define stochastic processes, SDEs, and pathwise divergences more concretely in \Cref{chapter:sde}.} under the calculus of stochastic processes, specifically addressing SDEs evolving in different directions of time. This theoretical innovation allows us to demonstrate that each aforementioned method can be viewed as the minimisation of a \emph{pathwise} divergence between probability measures.

We will therefore dedicate \Cref{chapter:sde} to developing the requisite theory of stochastic calculus, before proposing our framework in \Cref{chapter:cmcd} and showing how many existing algorithms are specific instantiations of our framework. Leveraging this framework's flexibility, we then develop a new state-of-the-art sampling algorithm, called Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusions (CMCD), which we will demonstrate to have strong theoretical guarantees while being practical and efficient. 

\subsection{A Unifying Framework for Conditional Generative Modelling}

Another critical facet of sampling algorithms is the ability to condition on specific observations or constraints. In language generation, for instance, one might condition on a prompt; in drug design, on specific chemical properties. More broadly, \emph{Bayesian inference itself is a conditional sampling task}: seeking draws from a model's posterior distribution \emph{conditioned} on observed data. Over the years, various algorithms have adapted diffusion models for this, from simple empirical techniques like Classifer-Free Guidance (CFG) to sophisticated approaches such as Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS). However, a consistent mathematical framework to unify these diverse methods and illuminate their underlying trade-offs has been missing, many of which manipulate an underlying stochastic process.

This thesis develops such a framework. In \Cref{chapter:deft}, we leverage Doob’s \(h\)-transform, a seminal property of SDEs, to introduce a novel stochastic-calculus formulation that reframes a wide array of conditional samplers as specific instances of this transform. This unifying lens allows us to derive \emph{Doob’s \(h\)-transform Efficient Fine-Tuning} (DEFT), an algorithm combining strong theoretical guarantees with practical efficiency. We demonstrate DEFT across tasks ranging from controlled image generation to protein design, establishing new state-of-the-art performance.

\subsection{Stochastic Sampling Algorithms for Large-Scale Bayesian Inference}

While the preceding sections focused on establishing unifying frameworks for generative and conditional sampling rooted in stochastic calculus--where stochastic processes modelled the dynamic evolution of samples--the second part of this thesis marks a shift in both application and algorithmic approach. We now concentrate on a critical application domain: large-scale Bayesian inference and explore how stochastic optimisation, a cornerstone of modern deep learning, can revolutionize sampling in this context. Here, stochastic processes will feature prominently again, but in a different capacity: as powerful, infinite-dimensional priors.

We fill focus particularly on Gaussian Processes (GPs), which allow us to build flexible Bayesian models and tap into rich theoretical results like \emph{pathwise conditioning}, which describes how samples from a GP prior can be transformed into posterior samples through a correction factor, albeit at a high computational cost. Confronting this cost, and inspired by the empirical success of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) in deep learning, we then ask: \emph{can pathwise conditioning algorithms be efficiently solved using SGD for the purpose of posterior sampling?}

In \Cref{chapter:gp_sampling}, we answer affirmatively for GPs. We re-cast pathwise conditioning as a quadratic optimization problem, amenable to low-variance SGD estimators, thereby developing sampling algorithms with \emph{near-linear} time complexity. This not only facilitates efficient posterior sampling but also enables a novel pathwise gradient estimation technique for GP hyperparameter optimisation, yielding significant speed-ups (up to 72x in some cases).

Subsequently, in \Cref{chapter:bnn_sampling}, we extend this SGD-centric sampling philosophy to Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs). By first employing the Linearised Laplace Approximation (LLA), we connect BNNs to equivalent Gaussian Process models. The core challenge then becomes scaling inference and hyperparameter selection for these massive linearised NNs. We address this by developing a sample-based Expectation-Maximisation (EM) algorithm. For the E-step, we perform posterior sampling using SGD with a low-variance stochastic objective. For the M-step, we introduce a novel method for hyperparameter selection that only requires access to these posterior samples. This combined approach allows us to perform both inference and hyperparameter selection for large BNNs by solving a series of quadratic optimisation problems using SGD, thereby avoiding explicit cubic costs. This enables high-quality uncertainty estimation and model selection at an ImageNet scale, demonstrating the power of combining stochastic process priors with scalable stochastic optimisation.

\section{A Guide on Reading the Thesis}

We dedicate \Cref{chapter:sampling} to an exploration of the sampling problem, discussing many existing algorithms across the fields of unnormalised density sampling, generative modelling, and conditional sampling. Throughout this chapter, we show how different algorithms are specific instantiations (or approximations) of an underlying stochastic calculus framework. Inspired by these connections, we dedicate \Cref{chapter:sde} to developing the requisite theory of stochastic calculus. Starting from a measure-theoretic framework, we eventually discuss \emph{pathwise} divergences and \emph{pathwise} probability ratios, before discussing the inherent issues in trying to define divergences between SDEs travelling in opposite directions in time. In \Cref{chapter:cmcd}, we develop a novel \emph{pathwise} probability density ratio, allowing us to define our hierarchical variational framework that unifies many existing generative modelling and sampling algorithms. In this chapter, we further develop our novel sampling algorithm, called Controlled Monte Carlo Diffusions (CMCD). \Cref{chapter:deft} is dedicated to a unifying framework for conditional generative modelling, leading to the development of a new, state-of-the-art algorithm called Doob's $h$-transform Efficient Fine-tuning (DEFT). \Cref{chapter:gp_sampling} and \Cref{chapter:bnn_sampling} are dedicated to the development of a family of algorithms that can efficiently sample from Gaussian Process and BNN posteriors using stochastic optimisation techniques, and use these samples to tune model hyperparameters, providing a highly scalable approach to model selection.

Throughout the thesis, we mention theorems, definitions, and other mathematical concepts with their relevant references in the following environment:

% Up to this point we have leveraged stochastic calculus to develop a unifying
% view of generative modelling, sampling, and conditional sampling.  Yet
% stochastic processes play an equally fundamental rôle in another corner of
% machine learning: \emph{Bayesian nonparametrics} (BNP).
% In BNP, an \emph{infinite–dimensional} stochastic process is pressed into
% service as a prior, thereby side-stepping the rigid finite parameterisations
% that often hamstring classical Bayesian models.  The Gaussian process (GP) is
% arguably the canonical example: by placing a GP prior on a latent function
% $f(\cdot)$ we obtain a joint Gaussian distribution over \emph{every} possible
% set of function evaluations, no matter how many or where they are queried.
% Crucially, the very mathematics of stochastic processes that we exploited in
% Chapters~\ref{chapter:sde}–\ref{chapter:deft} now becomes the bridge to a
% different goal: \emph{scalable Bayesian inference}.

% \paragraph{Why BNP—and why now?}
% The recent explosion of data and model capacity has renewed interest in
% methods that can grow their complexity with the evidence.  BNP meets that need
% in principle, but in practice its infinite-dimensional integrals are daunting:
% exact GP inference scales cubically in the number of observations and even
% \emph{drawing a single posterior sample} naively requires an $O(N^{3})$ matrix
% factorisation.  From the decision-theoretic standpoint articulated by
% \citet{Schervish1995} and the pragmatism championed by
% \citet{Ghahramani2012}, computation is therefore the binding constraint.

% \paragraph{A sampling-centric view of BNP.}
% Rather than approximate the posterior analytically (e.g.\ variationally) we
% take the same stance that underpinned our treatment of diffusion models:
% \emph{high-quality samples are a sufficient currency}.  Once a representative
% set of samples is available, posterior prediction, model comparison, marginal-
% likelihood estimation, and even hyper-parameter tuning can all be reduced to
% Monte Carlo estimators—precisely the toolkit refined in the first half of this
% thesis.

% \paragraph{Stochastic optimisation as a sampler.}
% The bottleneck is therefore no longer conceptual but algorithmic: how do we
% \emph{sample} efficiently from a high-dimensional Gaussian posterior?  The key
% insight is to cast GP sampling itself as a stochastic optimisation problem.
% By interpreting the Cholesky solve underlying GP posteriors as the stationary
% point of a quadratic objective, we show that carefully pre-conditioned
% \emph{stochastic gradient descent} (SGD) can replace $O(N^{3})$ factorizations
% with \emph{near-linear} time iteration.  Each SGD trajectory is a path in
% function space, and the same stochastic calculus tools—Itô’s lemma, martingale
% arguments, and pathwise divergences—that unified diffusion‐based generative
% models now certify the convergence and bias–variance trade-offs of our GP
% samplers.

% GP-sampling algorithms into the parameter space of large networks we obtain
% scalable Bayesian deep-learning models—an outcome that would be difficult to
% reach from a purely variational or purely MCMC standpoint alone.
